A B S T R A C T
The growing demand for high-performance lithium-ion batteries, especially in electric vehicles, has increased the focus on optimizing the production of nickel-rich 
cathode materials such as NMC811. This study investigates the synthesis of LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) via coprecipitation as oxalates, focusing on the effect of 
metal concentration (0.2 M, 1 M, 2 M) and reaction time (6 h, 24 h). The materials were characterized using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), X-Ray Diffraction 
(XRD), Inductively Coupled Plasma spectroscopy, and Particle Size Analysis. Electrochemical tests, including cycling stability and rate capability, assessed the impact 
of synthesis conditions on performance.
Results show that both metal concentration and coprecipitation reaction time significantly influence particle morphology, size distribution, crystallinity, and 
electrochemical behavior. The final cathode material is strongly affected by these parameters, showing a marked memory of the precursor’s properties. Materials 
synthesized at 2 M for 24 h exhibited the highest specific capacity and excellent capacity retention across a wide range of C-rates. In contrast, lower concentrations or 
shorter times yielded inferior performance, with reduced capacity and greater fading. This study demonstrates that careful control of synthesis conditions enables the 
development of nickel-rich NMC811 with high electrochemical performance, offering valuable insights for improving lithium-ion battery manufacturing.

1. Introduction
The global demand for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) has surged in 
recent years, driven primarily by the automotive industry’s transition to 
hybrid electric vehicles and electric vehicles. As a result, the use of LIBs 
has expanded across a wide range of applications. However, the devel­
opment of cathode materials remains partially constrained from the 
development and full-scale transfer of synthesis processes capable of 
ensuring the chemical-physical and morphological characteristics that 
guarantee high-performance materials for batteries coprecipitation.
Production of cathodic precursors by coprecipitation allows the 
obtainment of uniform, morphologically consistent, and spherical par­
ticles with a well-defined target size, as these properties significantly 
influence the performance of the final electrode material [1]. An 
improperly produced precursor, with particles that are irregular or too 
small, may lead to excessive void formation, ultimately reducing energy 
density by decreasing the amount of active material per unit volume [2,
3]. Coprecipitation offers the advantage of modulating the properties of 
solid particles by adjusting key operational parameters such as tem­
perature, pH, concentration, stirring rate, and reaction time. Currently, 
mixed hydroxide coprecipitation is the predominant method for pro­
ducing cathode materials [1]. For this production, the optimal reaction 
time has been identified to be between 12 and 24 h, the best temperature 
range for coprecipitation is between 50 and 60 ◦C, the ideal pH is 
approximately around 11, and the optimal concentration of metals in 
solution is 2 M [4–13]. However, this process is expensive and complex, 
requiring inert atmospheres to prevent manganese oxidation and the use 
of complexing agents to ensure the formation of a single mixed phase [4,
5]. As an alternative, coprecipitation as oxalates has been explored, 
offering a potentially more efficient and simplified approach.
Recent studies on layered transition-metal oxides have shown that 
even small variations in precursor chemistry can propagate during 
calcination and influence key structural features of the final cathode, 
including layer spacing, phase stability, and ion-transport behavior [16,
17]. These works support the broader principle that the chemical 
environment established during precursor formation can imprint a 
structural “memory” that affects the ordering, defect formation, and 
electrochemical response of layered oxide materials. This perspective 
further underscores the importance of systematically examining how 
coprecipitation parameters govern the morphology and structural order 
of Ni-rich NMC materials.
In coprecipitation as oxalates, oxalate ions serve as both the 
precipitating and chelating agents, forming metal complexes that slow 
the rate of precipitation and avoiding the application of harmful NH4OH 
that can generate NH3 at high pH [18]. Additionally, transition metals 
such as nickel, cobalt and manganese, and their mixtures form stable 
oxalate dihydrates across a wide range of mixing ratios, generating 
high-purity precursors and the use of inert atmosphere is not necessary 
[19].
There are relatively few articles in literature on the production of 
cathode materials using oxalic acid as a precipitating agent [20–23] and 
there is still no consensus on the optimal parameters. The literature 
reports a wide range of coprecipitation times, varying from 40 min to 20 
h [24,25], without providing explanations for this variability, consid­
ering that the precipitation kinetics of oxalates are almost instantaneous. 
Furthermore, the initial metal concentrations in the solution are often 
not specified. These factors are crucial for assessing the feasibility of a 
process as both metal concentration in solution and precipitation time 
directly influence the process productivity. In addition, the use of initial 
concentration larger than the one typically obtained by leaching of black 
mass in recycling process could determine the adoption of costly con­
centration steps [24].
In this study, the optimization of NMC811 cathode material pro­
duction was primarily focused on the influence of Ni, Mn, and Co con­
centration during the coprecipitation step. The effect of metals 
concentration, reaction time, and pH was evaluated to assess the 
feasibility of direct coprecipitation of NMC from leach liquor of LIB 
black masses [26]. The impact of the investigated factors was assessed 
by solid characterization (acid digestion for metal composition, Scan­
ning Electron Microscopy (SEM) for morphology, X-Ray Diffraction 
(XRD) for phase assessment, Raman spectroscopy for structural order, 
and particle size analyzer for size distribution). Electrochemical tests, 
including cycling stability and rate capability, were conducted to assess 
their influence on the performance of the final cathode material.
2. Experimental
2.1. LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 production
Three solutions with metals concentration of 0.2, 1, 2 M were pre­
pared by dissolving NiSO4 • 6H2O (98 %, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, 
MA, USA), CoSO4 • 7H2O (98 % VWR Chemicals, Radnor, PA, USA), 
MnSO4 • H2O (99 %, Thermo Scientific, Saint Louis, MO, USA) main­
taining Ni:Mn:Co molar ratio to 8:1:1 in each solution. Oxalic acid 1.5 M 
(Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) was used as precipitating agent. Initially, 
experiments were conducted at the spontaneous pH of the resulting 
solution, without pH control. Subsequently, a 5 M NaOH solution was 
used to adjust the pH to the desired value. The coprecipitation process 
was carried out in a reactor equipped with an external jacket, through 
which hot water circulated from a thermal bath, maintaining the system 
at 60 ◦C throughout the entire process. A mechanical stirrer was set to 
operate at a speed of 1000 rpm. Once the full volume of oxalic acid had 
been added, the pH was adjusted to 6.5 using 5 M NaOH (Emsure 
Supelco, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and the reaction was allowed to 
proceed for different times (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 24 h). The resulting res­
idue was filtered using a rotary vacuum pump. The samples were then 
placed in graduated tubes, centrifuged, and washed with water and 
ethanol. Subsequently, the centrifuged and washed samples were dried 
in an oven at 60 ◦C for 24 h (Table 1). The precursors underwent 
annealing step at 500 ◦C for 5 h with a ramp of 2 ◦C/min. Lithium hy­
droxide monohydrate (LiOH•H2O) was added to 10 % in excess of the 
stoichiometric value to the post annealing oxide and ground manually in 
an agate mortar. The grounded samples were placed in alumina crucible 
and calcined using a muffle (L3/11/B410, Nabertherm GmbH, Lilien­
thal, Germany). The calcination conditions were first heating to 500 ◦C 
for 6 h with a temperature ramp of 2 ◦C/min and second heating to 
850 ◦C for 12h with a temperature ramp of 3 ◦C/min.

Spectral acquisition and data processing were conducted using 
RENISHAW WiRE 4.4 and Origin Pro 2022 software platforms.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Chemical composition
The chemical composition of the precursors and cathode materials 
was analyzed to assess whether the stoichiometric ratios of the target 
metals (Ni, Co, Mn) in solution were maintained in the precipitated 
solids. Initial tests were carried out both at the solution’s spontaneous 
pH and under pH control at 6.5 over 2–24 h. Initial tests using precipi­
tation at the spontaneous pH of the solution (2.0 M, 1.0 M, and 0.2 M 
corresponding to pH 1.80, 2.05, and 2.90, respectively) indicated a 
manganese deficiency in the precursors. Plotting the stoichiometric ra­
tios of nickel, cobalt, and manganese over the time revealed that the 
relative concentrations of all three metals remained stable throughout 
the experiment, with no significant deviations observed during the 
analyzed time intervals (2–24 h) (Fig. 1). pH control during precipita­
tion at a metal concentration of 2 M resulted in an average increase of 
30 % in manganese precipitation, with a standard deviation of 4 % 
calculated from six measurements at different precipitation times. When 
the metal concentration was reduced to 1 M, pH control led to a 16 % 
increase in manganese precipitation (standard deviation: 4 %). At a 
concentration of 0.2 M, the effect of pH control was minimal, with only a 
0.6 % increase observed and a standard deviation of 0.3 %.
This behavior can be explained by the fact that the effect of pH 
adjustment is less pronounced at low concentrations, since the sponta­
neous pH is already relatively high. In contrast, at higher concentrations 
(1 M and 2 M), the beneficial effect of pH control is mainly attributable 
to the reduced solubility of manganese oxalate as the pH approaches 6.5. 
By maintaining a constant pH during the coprecipitation process, the 
desired stoichiometric ratios for the precursor were then successfully 
achieved.
Chemical composition by acid digestion of the cathodic materials 
obtained after thermal treatment with lithium (Table 2) confirmed as 
expected the preservation of the target Ni, Co, and Mn stoichiometry.
3.2. Morphology and structure
The precursors’ characterization by SEM evidenced the obtainment 
of agglomerated particles (secondary particles) (Fig. 2c–h) made up of 
anisotropic lamellar-shaped particles (primary particles) (Fig. 2a and b). 
The primary particles resemble the first nuclei of crystallization, then 
growing by increasing the thickness in the direction perpendicular to the 
plane. This mechanism of formation is typical of precipitation processes 
including nucleation, growth, aggregation and cementation of aggre­
gates by direct growth to give stable agglomerate [27].
Particle size distribution evidenced that, in all investigated cases, the 
volume-based distribution is bimodal (Fig. 3a). The secondary particles 
associated with the second peak are predominant by volume (and then 
mass) and exhibit modes ranging from 3.6 to 18.5 μm as a function of the 
initial concentration and the coprecipitation time, indicating a marked 
dependence on both parameters (Table 3).
Considering these secondary particles, at 2 M, the mode increases 
from 11.2 μm at 6 h to 13.3 μm at 24 h; at 1 M, it increases from 17.5 μm 
to 18.5 μm over the same time interval. Conversely, at 0.2 M, extending 
the coprecipitation time leads to a significant reduction in the size of the

μm), largely unaffected by reaction time and reactant concentration, 
indicates a growth arrest and the lack of further aggregation that are not 
due to depletion of metals in solution. In addition, if this sub-population 
were to emerge from fragmentation phenomena, the size distribution 
should change with the operating conditions, especially with reaction 
time. Instead, this population seems to reflect a self-regulating growth 
mechanism in which particles reach a maximum stable size. This phe­
nomenon has already been observed in other systems, explaining such 
self-limiting growth by stabilization due to adsorption phenomena or 
minimization of surface energy [28–30]. Once this non-aggregating size 
is attained, further assembly does not occur.
According to this hypothesis, lamellar primary particles that proceed 
to aggregation should remain below this size threshold [27–29].
In order to verify this hypothesis image analysis of SEM micrographs 
(Fig. S2 a–b) was performed to assess the typical size of primary particles 
aggregating. Image analysis reveals clear trends in the thickness of the 
lamella-shaped primary particles as a function of metal concentration 
showing that at 2 M (Fig. 2a) the primary particles are smaller (with a 
thickness of about 200 nm) than at 0.2 M (Fig. 2b) (400 nm). At the 
intermediate concentration of 1 M, the primary particles appear less 
homogeneous.
Geometric considerations confirm that these thicknesses are below 
the equivalent diameter of the non-aggregating population (~0.85 μm), 
supporting the hypothesis that only thinner lamellae can participate in 
aggregation.
The observed difference in characteristic thickness depending on 
metal concentration could be explained in terms of ionic- 
strength–dependent double-layer screening: at higher ionic strength (2 
M concentration), electrostatic repulsion is more effectively screened, 
allowing lamellae to aggregate at smaller thicknesses. Conversely, at 
lower ionic strength (0.2 M concentration), weaker screening stabilizes 
thicker lamellae before aggregation occurs [31].
Then according to the experimental findings, there is an optimal size 
of primary particles to have aggregation, and this size depends on metal 
concentration in solution.
This is in agreement with the modeling of aggregation phenomena 
according to population balance, assuming an agglomeration kernel as 
the product of two factors: collision efficiency and collision frequency. 
For very small lamellar primary particles, collision efficiency is high, but 
collision frequency is low, leading to negligible aggregation. As thick­
ness increases, collision frequency rises while efficiency decreases, 
yielding an optimal size range for aggregation [32].
Considering the SEM micrographs (Fig. 2a and b) other effects of 
concentration can be assessed on nucleation and growth. At high con­
centration (2 M), nucleation predominates, yielding numerous thin 
lamellae that rapidly reach the minimum aggregating thickness (~200 
nm). At low concentration (0.2 M) nucleation is slower and lamellae 
stabilize at a larger thickness (~400 nm) before aggregation becomes 
possible (Fig. 4 sections “primary particles’ formation” and “secondary 
particles’ formation”).

cementation (by direct growth) in order to have mechanically stable 
agglomerate. Then, residual ions in solution deposit at interparticle 
contacts and within fissures, simultaneously reinforcing junctions and 
filling voids (Fig. 4 section “secondary particles’ ripening”). When the

near-spherical morphologies. At intermediate concentrations (1 M), 
extensive nucleation consumes most ions, leaving too little metal in 
solution to sustain neck thickening and void filling; aggregates therefore 
remain porous, irregular, and mechanically fragile [33,34]. In the same 
way, at low concentration the lack of reactants in solution limits 
cementation of secondary particles in stable agglomerates.
During ripening different maturation effect were observed according 
to initial concentration (Fig. 4 section “secondary particles’ ripening). At 
high concentration, continued ion availability supports the growth of 
secondary particles via direct molecular attachment, accompanied by 
neck coarsening, pore collapse, and surface rounding. At intermediate 
concentration, maturation arrests early due to reactant scarcity; and at 
low concentration, the initial lack of effective cementation renders 
interparticle necks unstable, so over long times aggregates undergo neck 
thinning and breakup, leading to net disintegration rather than 
compaction.
SEM images of the cathodic materials obtained after thermal treat­
ment with lithium (Fig. 5), revealed notable changes with respect to 
precursors. The spherical morphology observed in the precursor was 
lost, with the cathodic materials exhibiting an irregular morphology and 
a reduction in average size of secondary particles. This morphological 
deterioration is likely the result of suboptimal calcination conditions.
In literature, several calcination protocols have been proposed for 
layered oxide cathodes, yet no standardized or optimized procedure has 
been established, especially when starting from oxalate precursors. A 
first strategy involves a two-step route, where the oxalate precursor is 
pre-heated (annealing step) at ~450 ◦C in air to release CO2. Lithium is 
then added before a final high-temperature calcination [23]. This 
approach may help mitigate structural rearrangements during lithiation 
and favor better control over particle morphology. Alternatively, a 
direct two-step calcination in presence of lithium is commonly used, 
with an initial stage between 450 and 600 ◦C for decomposition and 
partial lithiation, followed by a second stage between 750 and 850 ◦C for 
full crystallization [20–22].
However, a clear consensus on the optimal temperatures, heating 
ramps, and durations is still lacking, despite their critical role in con­
trolling particle morphology, phase formation, and electrochemical 
performance [35,36].
The particle size distributions of the cathode materials, shown in 
Fig. 3b, reveal that the dimensional differences observed in the pre­
cursors are no longer present in the secondary particles of the cathode 
materials. High-magnification SEM analysis (Fig. 5a–f) revealed that all 
cathode materials are composed of nanoscale primary particles that 
agglomerate into larger secondary particles. Although the secondary 
morphology of the calcined powders is not preserved from the precursor 
stage, specific nanoscale features imprinted during coprecipita­
tion—such as primary-particle size and its dependence on concentration 
and residence time—persist after calcination. This behaviour indicates 
that the observed coprecipitation-driven memory effect belongs to the 
broader framework of structural heredity rather than macroscopic 
morphology retention.
Image analysis (Fig. S2 c-h) indicates that, after 6 h of coprecipita­
tion, the average size of NMC primary particles are 300 ± 150 nm, 450 
± 350 nm, and 460 ± 250 nm for precursor concentrations of 2 M, 1 M, 
and 0.2 M, respectively. Extending the coprecipitation time to 24 h the 
average sizes increase to 340 ± 120 nm, 640 ± 300 nm, and 660 ± 270 
nm for the same concentrations. These results are consistent with the 
proposed nucleation and growth model, whereby primary particles grow 
larger as the metal concentration decreases. Even after calcination, this 
trend persists: the primary particles in cathodic materials obtained with 
high concentrations are smaller than the ones obtained at low concen­
tration, and this effect is further amplified by the coprecipitation reac­
tion time.
An interesting aspect to highlight is that the coprecipitation reaction 
time appears to significantly influence the particle size distribution of 
the cathode material. Specifically, all materials produced with a 24-h 
coprecipitation time exhibit a markedly narrower size distribution, 
with Dx90 values of 14.3 μm, 21.1 μm, and 24.0 μm for NMC at 2 M, 1 M, 
and 0.2 M, respectively, compared to those produced with a 6-h 
coprecipitation time, which show Dx90 values of 42.8 μm, 36.0 μm, 
and 33.2 μm for NMC at 2 M, 1 M, and 0.2 M. Notably, the cathode 
material derived from a 2 M precursor concentration with a 24-h 
coprecipitation time exhibits the narrowest particle size distribution, 
with a Dx90 of 14.29 μm.

in the calcined materials is fully coherent with what was previously 
described for the precursor stage: low metal concentrations lead to 
slower nucleation kinetics and inherently broader primary-particle

Uniform spherical particles of defined size are critical for the final

mercial reference cathode material, were structurally characterized by 
using XRD (Fig. S3, Fig. 6).
The resulting diffractograms of the produced materials were found to 
be completely superimposable with those of the commercial reference 
material. As shown in Fig. 6, both the produced NMC811 and com­
mercial NMC811 exhibit eight identical peaks, with matching positions 
and relative intensities. This indicates that the produced material suc­
cessfully crystallizes in the reference layered hexagonal phase. These 
results confirm that the production of NMC811 via coprecipitation as 
oxalates was achieved without the formation of secondary phases. By 
analyzing the peak intensities, we can evaluate the extent of cation 
mixing during the calcination process by focusing on the I(003) to I(104) 
peak intensity (Table 2). Cation mixing is common issue in nickel-rich 
cathodes, arising from the similar ionic radii of lithium (0.76 Å) and 
nickel (0.69 Å), which can lead to the exchange of ions between their 
respective sites during calcination.
Typically, cation mixing is inversely proportional to the ratio of I 
(003)/I(104) ratio. For values of I(003)/I(104) greater than 1.2, it is 
generally accepted that no significant cation mixing occurs during 
calcination [37]. The data in Table 3 suggest coprecipitation time may 
influence this phenomenon.
Extending the coprecipitation time to 24 h limits the degree of nickel 
substitution by lithium during the calcination process. Statistical tests 
confirmed that precipitation time could have a significant effect on 
cation mixing (p = 0.036) (Fig. S4). This indicates that longer copreci­
pitation times help minimize unwanted ion interdiffusion, thereby 
improving the structural integrity of nickel-rich cathodes.
In order to assess further information about structure of the different 
cathodic materials Raman characterization was performed.
The Raman spectra, as shown in Fig. S5, present a broad main band 
extending between about 413 and 641 cm−1, attributed to O-M-O (ox­
ygen-metal-oxygen) type lattice vibrations, which are characteristic of 
transition metal oxide materials found in NMC cathodes. This band re­
flects the collective vibrations of the lamellar structure of the material 
and is in line with what has been reported for NMC compounds with 
high nickel content [38,39]. The localized peak around 1087 cm−1, 
present in almost all the samples, can be attributed to M-O-type 
stretching vibrations in local distorted domains or surface phases arising 
from carbonates or Li2CO3 residues formed during preparation or 
handling of the material [40,41]. The persistence of this signal in all 
samples suggests the presence of a comparable amount of such surface 
species. A first significant difference can be observed comparing NMC 
2M samples at different reaction times. In the NMC 2M 24h sample three 
additional low-frequency peaks emerge (ca. 96, 155 and 192 cm−1), the 
intensity of which is almost negligible in the NMC 2M 6h sample 
(Fig. S6). Such low-frequency peaks may be associated with the lattice 
modes of Li2CO3 [42]. Another difference between NMC 2M 24h and 
NMC 2M 6h lies in the components of the main band related to the NMC 
observed between 413 and 641 cm−1. This band consists of three 
partially overlapping peaks, which can be deconvoluted and are 
commonly assigned to the Eg and A1g modes associated with oxygen 
vibrations within the layered R3m structure. Variations in the position 
and intensity of these peaks provide valuable insight into the local 
structure and electrochemical state of the material. A shift in peak po­
sition may indicate changes in the oxidation state of the transition 
metals or lattice distortion, while a decrease in intensity or increased 
broadening often reflects higher structural disorder, cation mixing (e.g., 
Ni2+ in Li sites), or the formation of disordered surface phases. There­
fore, analyzing these spectral features allows us to assess the degree of 
crystallinity, the preservation of the layered structure, and the potential 
electrochemical performance of the electrode material. In our spectra, 
the main NMC band is composed of three broad peaks centered at 
approximately 469 cm−1, 543 cm−1, and 587 cm−1. The feature at 469 
cm−1 is attributed to the Eg mode, associated with in-plane O–M–O 
bending vibrations. The dominant contribution at 543 cm−1 corresponds 
to the symmetric A1g stretching mode of oxygen atoms against the 
transition-metal layers and is highly sensitive to the local oxidation state 
and the degree of structural order. The higher-frequency component at 
587 cm−1 is often linked to short-range distortions or asymmetric M −O 
stretching and may also include contributions from surface or 
defect-related vibrations. The relative intensity and sharpness of these 
components provide a direct fingerprint of structural integrity and 
degradation pathways in Ni-rich layered oxides. The main difference in 
the bands of the NMC was observed in sample NMC 1M 24h where there 
is a clear change (Fig. S7) in the structure of the compound while, in the 
other samples (Fig. S6 and S8), the structure seems to be always the 
same with little difference. Finally, by analyzing the intensities of the 
peaks present in all compounds, it was possible to see a trend that 
correlated with the electrochemical performance of the compounds 
(ordered as follows: NMC 2M 24h, NMC 0.2M 24h, NMC 0.2M 6h, NMC 
1M 6h, NMC 2M 6h, NMC 1M 24h). These differences, summarized in 
Fig. 7 and evident for the peak at 96 cm−1 and for the peak at 587 cm−1, 
are less obvious for the other peaks but show a pattern that could 
indicate the crystalline quality or the number of surface defects present 
in the various compounds providing an indicator for preliminary anal­
ysis and quality assessment of these materials.
3.3. Electrochemical characterization
The electrochemical performance of the produced LiNi0.8Mn0.1­
Co0.1O2 (NMC811) cathode materials was systematically evaluated 
through galvanostatic cycling and rate capability tests to assess the in­
fluence of both metal ion concentration in solution (0.2 M, 1 M, and 2 M) 
and coprecipitation reaction time (6 h vs. 24 h). Fig. 8(a–f) collectively 
demonstrate that these two parameters play a critical role in deter­
mining the specific capacity, capacity retention, and rate performance of 
the final electrode materials.
Fig. 8a shows the cycling performance of cathodes obtained from 
precursors synthesized with a 6-h coprecipitation time, while the 
coulombic efficiencies (CEs) from galvanostatic cycling are provided in

Fig. S12a. The NMC811 produced at a metal concentration of 0.2 M

shorter coprecipitation times are insufficient for producing structurally 
robust and electrochemically stable NMC particles, particularly at 
highest concentration, where poor precursor uniformity and smaller, 
irregular particles lead to accelerated capacity fading.
In contrast, Fig. 8b demonstrates the improvement in electro­
chemical performance when the coprecipitation reaction time is 
extended to 24 h. The 2 M sample, in this case, delivered a higher initial 
specific capacity (~200 mAh g−1) and maintained it with minimal 
degradation throughout the 100 cycles. Similarly, the 0.2 M sample 
exhibited good long-term stability even starting from lower initial ca­
pacity value (~150 mAh g−1), ending near 80 mAh g−1, while the 1 M 
material showed significantly reduced capacity stability compared to its 
6-h counterpart. Anomalous behavior of the 1 M material is further 
confirmed by its CE (Fig. S12b). In this case, a CE exceeding 100 % was 
observed, likely associated with irreversible reduction processes. Since 
the specific capacity of this material is particularly low, the relative 
contribution of such side reactions becomes proportionally more sig­
nificant compared, if present, to the other electrodes which may explain 
the anomalously high CE values. The enhanced performance of 2 M 
material is attributed to the improved crystallinity and homogeneity of 
the particles formed during prolonged coprecipitation, which translates 
into better structural integrity and lower material resistance during 
cycling. This increased crystallinity is reflected in the redox behavior of 
the 2 M material (Fig. S13b), which, unlike the other electrodes 
(Fig. S13a–b), exhibits oxidation peaks at 3.7 V, 4.0 V, and 4.2 V. These 
features correspond to the sequential multiphase transitions from hex­
agonal to monoclinic, monoclinic to hexagonal, and hexagonal to a 
higher-order hexagonal phase, respectively [43].
Fig. 8c and d shows the galvanostatic charge/discharge voltage 
profiles at the 20th cycle for all tested materials at a C/3 rate. These 
profiles provide insight into the electrochemical polarization and 
structural ordering of the materials. Cathodes produced at 2 M with 24 h 
coprecipitation (Fig. 8d) exhibit the most well-defined and symmetric 
voltage plateaus, indicative of lower overpotentials and improved phase 
homogeneity during cycling. In contrast, the 0.2 M and 1 M samples 
display broader and more sloped profiles, suggesting increased polari­
zation, possibly due to slower Li+ diffusion or higher surface impedance.
These findings highlight the synergistic benefit of high metal ion 
concentration (2 M) and extended coprecipitation time (24 h) in pro­
ducing cathode materials with excellent cycling durability and high- 
capacity retention. The observed capacity fading at shorter coprecipi­
tation times and lower precursor concentrations is consistent with 
literature reports showing that particle-size heterogeneity, high surface 
reactivity, and structural defects in Ni-rich layered oxides accelerate 
degradation pathways, including transition-metal dissolution and elec­
trolyte decomposition [44–47].

M materials show less recovery, indicating some permanent capacity 
loss. A marked improvement in performance is observed in Fig. 8 f), 
where materials produced with a 24-h coprecipitation time are high­

of nearly 200 mAh g−1 at C/10 and retains about 120 mAh g−1 at 2C,

4. Conclusions
This work demonstrates that the precipitation of nickel-rich NMC811 
oxalate precursors is strongly influenced by metal concentration and 
coprecipitation time, which govern the precipitation steps according to 
nucleation, growth, aggregation and agglomeration steps giving sec­
ondary particles as agglomerates of primary lamella-shaped particles. 
Image analysis of SEM micrographs revealed that concentration dictates 
the critical thickness of lamellar primary particles, which can aggregate 
(~200 nm at 2 M and ~400 nm at 0.2 M). Reaction time further mod­
ulates secondary particles’ evolution, driving compaction into near- 
spherical morphologies at high concentration, yielding porous and 
weakly cemented architectures under intermediate conditions, and 
aggregate disintegration at low concentration.
These results establish a mechanistic framework linking precursor 
structure and morphology to solution chemistry and reaction time.

high order of the structure as evidenced by X-ray diffraction and Raman

Overall, these results highlight that the electrochemical performance 
of NMC811 is governed by precursor-controlled microstructural features 
rather than external morphology alone. Fine-tuning coprecipitation 
parameters—specifically metal concentration and reaction time­
—allows simultaneous control over nucleation, growth, and aggrega­
tion, ultimately dictating the structural integrity and performance of the 
cathode material. Future optimization of the calcination process, 
particularly through controlled temperature ramps during CO2 release, 
may further preserve precursor-derived features and enhance cycling 
stability [48]. The simple, scalable use of oxalic acid without chelating 
agents or inert atmospheres underscores the potential of this approach 
for sustainable, cost-effective production of next-generation Li-ion 
cathodes.

An eﬀective co-modiﬁcation strategy to enhance
the cycle stability of LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 for
lithium-ion batteries†
Jingjing Zhou,a Bingxin Wei,b Meng Liu,a Yinping Qin,a Hongyu Cheng,a

Ni-rich cathode materials suﬀer from rapid capacity fading caused by interface side reactions and bulk
structure degradation. Previous studies show that Co is conducive to bulk structure stability and sulfate
can react with the residual lithium (LiOH and Li2CO3) on the surface of Ni-rich cathode materials and
form a uniform coating to suppress the side reactions between the cathode and electrolyte. Here,
CoSO4 is utilized as a modiﬁer for LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) cathode materials. It reacts with the
residual lithium on the surface of the NCM811 cathode to form Li-ion conductive Li2SO4 protective
layers and Co doping simultaneously during the high-temperature sintering process, which can suppress
the side reactions between the Ni-rich cathode and electrolyte and eﬀectively prevent the structural
transformation. As a result, the co-modiﬁed NCM811 cathode with 3 wt% CoSO4 exhibits an improved
cycling performance of 81.1% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 1C and delivers an excellent rate
performance at 5C of 187.4 mA h g−1, which is 10.2% higher than that of the pristine NCM811 cathode.

Introduction
Among the state-of-the-art electrochemical energy storage
devices, lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are the most promising and
world-changing energy storage devices, as they possess high
energy density and fast recharging capability. In order to meet
the ever-increasing desire for high energy density, Ni-rich
ternary cathode materials have become the most promising
candidates.1–4
However, they are still facing the shortcoming of rapid
capacity fading, which limit their practical application.5 These
drawbacks are caused by the following: Firstly, during cycling,
the anisotropic structure transformation leads to the structure
collapse, and the rock-salt type disordered surface structures
with a higher activation energy barrier for Li+ migration
decrease the electrochemical performance.6,7 Secondly, during
the charging process, Ni2+ and Ni3+ oxidize to Ni4+, which can
react much more easily with the electrolyte by parasitic
reactions.8,9 Thirdly, the residual lithium on the surface of the
material is easy to react with H2O and CO2 in the air to form
a Li2CO3/LiOH passivation layer, which not only increases the
interface impedance, but also leads to an irreversible capacity
loss during the rst cycle.10–12
To address the above issues, numerous methods, such as
surface coating and element doping, are widely used to enhance
the interface or bulk stability of Ni-rich cathode materials.13
Doping with a metallic element (Ti, Mg, Al, Co etc) into the bulk
crystallographic structure of Ni-rich materials is considered to be
a representative method to strengthen the structural stability of
the whole particle.14–18 Zhang et al. reported that Ti4+-doped Ni-
rich cathode LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 shows multiple performances
with high capacity, good rate performance and excellent cyclic
stability.14 Sun et al. reported that Mg and Al co-substituted
LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 have positive eﬀects on structure, electro-
chemical property and thermal stability.15 Guo et al. found that
Co doped LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cathode material shows high
capacity and maintain notable cycling stability in 300 cycles.17
Based on rst-principle calculation, Hu et al. speculated that Co
doping can increase the space distance of the Li layer, which is
benecial to improve the rate performance of Ni-rich cathode.18
Also, surface coating of Ni-rich materials by metal oxides (Al2O3,
TiO2, ZrO2),19–21 metal uorides (LiF, LaF3)22,23 and metal phos-
phates (Li3PO4)24 also has been widely reported. Lithium-ion
conductor can enhance the surface chemical and structural
stability, by acting as a protective layer that avoids direct reac-
tions between the active materials and the electrolyte compo-
nents.25 Aurbach et al. reported that Li2SO4 coating layer can

Methods
2.1
Preparation
The spherical precursor was prepared by the co-precipitation

10 h with owing O2, abbreviated as NCM811.
CoSO4-modied NCM811 was prepared by mixing CoSO4-
$7H2O and NCM811 under mortaring for 30 min. The mixtures
were calcined at 600, 700, 800 °C for 4 h with owing O2. The
content of CoSO4$7H2O in the mixture was 1, 3 and 5 wt%, and
the obtained samples were labeled as NCM811-CS-1, NCM811-
CS-3, and NCM811-CS-5. The equivalent molar amount of
CoC2O4$2H2O and Li2SO4 as modiers were replaced of 3 wt%
CoSO4
to
modify
NCM811
respectively.
Li2SO4-modied
NCM811 and CoC2O4-modied NCM811 were prepared under
the same condition of the NCM811-CS-3, which denoted as
NCM811-Li2SO4 and NCM811-CoC2O4 respectively.
2.2
Characterization
The morphology and the elements distribution of the prepared
samples were characterized using scanning electron micros-
copy (SEM, Hitachi Regulus 8230). X-ray-diﬀraction (XRD)
patterns of all samples was characterized using PANalytical
Empyrean, from 10° to 90°, with Cu Ka (l = 1.5406 Å) radiation.
The elemental analysis of pristine NCM811 and NCM811-CS-
3 were investigated by inductively coupled plasma optical
emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Agilent 5110) and inductively
coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800). The
components Li, Mn, Ni, and Co were measured by ICP-OES and
S was measured by ICP-MS. The compositions of pristine and
modied NCM811 electrodes were characterized using an
ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray photoelectron spectrometer aided with
Ar+ ion etching.
2.3
Electrochemical testing
Electrochemical performance was measured by CR2032 coin-
type cells. Cathodes were prepared by 80 wt% of active mate-
rial, 10 wt% of Super-p and 10 wt% of poly(vinylideneuoride)
(PVDF), which were mixed with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP)
as the slurry, and then taking the slurry to coat on an Al current
collector, and roll-pressed. The cells were assembled using
lithium metal as the anode, microporous membranes (Celgard
2550s) as a separator, 1 mol L−1 LiPF6 in a mixed solution
containing ethylene carbonate (EC)/dimethyl carbonate (DMC)/
ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) with volume ratio of 1 : 1 : 1 as
electrolyte. All cell assemblies were performed in an Ar-lled
MBraun glovebox, where humidity and oxygen were controlled
to less than 1 ppm. The cycling performance, rate capability and
galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) test were
performed using a LANHE CT2001A battery tester (Wuhan,
China) at room temperature under various rates. 1C rate
corresponds to a current density of 200 mA h g−1 in this work.
The duration time for each applied galvanostatic current (0.1C)
and relaxation during the GITT measurements was 0.5 and 4 h,
respectively. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was
measured on a Solartron analytical 1470 Cell Test System in
a frequency range of 0.1–105 Hz. And conductivities of pristine
and modied NCM811 materials under diﬀerent pressures were
also tested on the Solartron Cell Test System.

displays that the S–O (169.5 eV) peak in S 2p is still strong on the
surface of NCM811-CS-3 aer 200 cycles (Fig. S9 and Table S2†).
These indicate the Li2SO4 coating layer displays good stability at
the high voltage of 4.5 V vs. Li+/Li for long cycling. In addition,
EDS was used to test the content of transition metal elements
deposited on lithium metal surface of pristine and modied
NCM811 aer 200 cycles. As shown in Fig. S10,† the dissolution
of Mn and Ni elements were detected on the lithium metal side of
NCM811 aer 200 cycles, which were not be detected of the
modied NCM811, indicating that co-modication can inhibit
the dissolution of transition metals during the long-term cycling.
4
Conclusion
In summary, the co-modied LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cathode
materials with Li2SO4 coating and Co-doping were prepared by
a facile and eﬀective one-step strategy using CoSO4 as a modier
in this work. With adding 3 wt% CoSO4, NCM811-CS-3 shows
the excellent cyclic stability (with 81.1% capacity retention in
200 cycles at 1C) and rate performance (187.4 mA h g−1 at 5C).
The formation of Li2SO4 surface coating layer consumed the
residual lithium compounds on the surface of Ni-rich cathode
material and mitigated the side reactions between cathode
materials and electrolyte. Meanwhile, the Co doping can
improve the stability of the structure and promote Li+ ion
diﬀusion. Thus, co-modied Ni-rich cathode materials deliver
an excellent electrochemical performance. And the results
indicate that this co-modication strategy can eﬀectively
improve the electrochemical performance. And the results
indicate that this co-modication strategy can also be expected
to apply to other oxide cathode materials.

ABSTRACT
High‐nickel cathode, LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811), and sulfide‐solid electrolyte are a promising combination for all‐solid‐
state lithium batteries (ASSLBs). However, this combination faces the issue of interfacial instability between the cathode and
electrolyte. Given the surface alkalinity of NCM811, we propose a strategy to construct a solid–polymer–electrolyte (SPE)
interphase on NCM811 surface by leveraging the surface alkaline residues to nucleophilically initiate the in‐situ ring‐opening
polymerization of cyclic organic molecules. As a proof‐of‐concept, this study demonstrates that the ring‐opening
copolymerization of 1,3‐dioxolane and maleic anhydride produces a homogeneous, compact, and conformal SPE layer on
NCM811 surface to prevent the cathode from contact and reaction with Li6PS5Cl solid‐state electrolyte. Consequently, the SPE‐
modified‐NCM811 in ASSLBs exhibits high capacities of 193.5 mA h g–1 at 0.2 C, 160.9 mA h g–1 at 2.0 C and 112.3 mA h g–1 at
10 C, and particularly, excellent long‐term cycling stabilities over 11000 cycles with a 71.95% capacity retention at 10 C at 25°C,
as well as a remained capacity of 117.9 mA h g–1 after 8000 cycles at 30 C at 60°C, showing a great application prospect. This
study provides a new route for creating electrochemically and structurally stable solid–solid interfaces for ASSLBs.

Introduction
All‐solid‐state lithium batteries (ASSLBs) have a great potential
to surpass the current Li‐ion batteries (LIBs) in safety and en-
ergy density and are therefore considered as a prospective en-
ergy
storage
technology
for
portable
electronics,
electric
vehicles, and energy storage devices [1–3]. The electrochemical
performances of ASSLBs are highly relative to the types of solid‐
state electrolytes (SEs) and cathode materials and the stabilities
of SE–electrode interfaces [4–6]. To enable an ASSLB, various
SEs have been developed in the past decades, including
inorganic solid electrolytes (oxides, sulfides, and halides)
[7–10],
solid
polymer
electrolytes
[11–13],
and
organic–
inorganic hybrid electrolytes [14–17]. Among these SEs, sulfide‐
based SEs exhibit great application promise in ASSLBs due to
their
very
high
room‐temperature
ionic
conductivity
(~10–2 S cm–1), low gravimetric density, and soft mechanical
properties [18–20]. Besides, for achieving a high energy density,
high‐Ni
layered
oxides
with
large
specific
capacity
(> 200 mA h g–1) and high operation voltage (~4.3 V), such as

LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811), have been widely investigated
as cathode materials of sulfide‐based ASSLBs [21–23]. However,
the ASSLBs based on this combination usually have difficulty
demonstrating satisfactory cyclability and rate capability, pri-
marily due to the poor interfacial stability between the cathode
and electrolyte.
The unstable NCM811 cathode/sulfide electrolyte interface mainly
arises from the following two aspects. (1) The narrow electro-
chemical stability window of sulfide SEs (1.7–2.1 V, vs. Li+/Li)
[24, 25]. The sulfide SEs are prone to oxidatively decompose into
electrochemically inert products with poor electronic and ionic
conductivities on NCM811 cathode at high charging potential,
resulting in a ceaseless increase of interface resistance during cy-
cling [26–28]. (2) The poor physical contact between sulfide SEs
and active material particles due to the rigidity of NCM811. More
significantly, this interfacial contact inevitably becomes even
worse during repeated charge/discharge owing to the volume
changes of NCM811 particles [29, 30]. To stabilize the cathode/-
electrolyte interface, the NCM811 particles were usually coated
with an inorganic layer to prevent their contact with sulfide‐SEs.
The inorganic coatings for this purpose include Al2O3 [31], ZrO2
[32], Li3PO4 [33], LixTaOxF5−x [34], LiNbO3 [35, 36], etc. However,
the formation of these surface coatings generally involves sophis-
ticated processes and harsh reaction conditions, such as the sol‐gel
process and atomic layer deposition [37]. In addition, these coat-
ings are easily destroyed by the volume changes of NCM811
particles during Li+ intercalation/extraction cycles due to the
inherent rigidity and fragility of the inorganic layer [38–40].
Therefore, it is essential to create an electrochemically and struc-
turally stable interphase with complete morphology, flexible
mechanical property, and high ionic conductivity for stabilizing
the interface of NCM811/sulfide electrolyte.
Such a multifunctional interphase can be constructed by in‐situ
formation of a polymeric Li+‐conducting layer on the NCM811
surface. Our previous studies have demonstrated that a con-
formal polymeric Li+‐conductive layer can be conveniently
formed on the sulfur/carbon (S/C) cathode during cycling by
the nucleophilic reaction of polysulfide intermediates with vi-
nylene carbonate (VC) molecules in ether‐based electrolyte [41].
In this reaction, polysulfide anions serve as a nucleophilic
reagent to initiate the ring‐opening polymerization of VC mo-
lecules, generating a Li+‐conductive polymer layer. Considering
that there exist some amounts of alkaline residues (or sites)
containing strong nucleophilic groups, such as OH−and CO3
2−
on the NCM811 surface, we propose to construct a polymer
coating layer on the NCM particles surface by the in‐situ ring‐
opening polymerization of cyclic monomers at the nucleophilic
sites. Since NCM811 material has the same grain‐surface
chemistry with uniformly distributed alkaline sites, the result-
ing polymer layer should be homogeneous and conformal and,
therefore, capable of serving as an electrochemically and
structurally stable interphase of composite cathode for ASSLBs.
In this study, we selected 1,3‐dioxolane (DOL) and maleic anhy-
dride (MA) as a mixed precursor of in‐situ polymerization to form
a solid‐polymer‐electrolyte (SPE) coating on the single‐crystal
NCM811 (SPE‐NCM811). Since the DOL‐MA copolymer is
mechanically
flexible,
electronically
insulating,
and
Li+‐
conductive, the thus‐formed SPE coating is allowed to closely
contact and merge with sulfide SEs to create a stable interlocking
interface during cold pressing of cathode preparation, thus sepa-
rating the direct contact, preventing the side reactions, and facil-
itating the rapid transfer of Li+ ions between NCM811 particles
and sulfide‐SEs. As a consequence, the SPE coating greatly en-
hances the interfacial stability and cycling stability of NCM811
cathode in a typical sulfide‐SE of Li6PS5Cl (LPSCl). When cycled
in the ASSLB with a LiIn anode and LPSCl electrolyte, the SPE‐
NCM811 exhibited high capacities of 193.5 mA h g–1 at 0.2 C,
160.9 mA h g–1 at 2.0 C and 112.3 mA h g–1 at 10 C, and also ex-
cellent long‐term cycling stabilities with a 71.95% capacity reten-
tion over 11,000 cycles at 10 C at 25°C, as well as a remained
capacity of 117.9 mA h g–1 after 8000 cycles at 30 C at 60°C. Even
at a high areal loading of 38.7 mg cm–2, the SPE‐NCM811 still
demonstrated a considerably high capacity of 203.9 mA h g–1 at
0.1 C
at
60°C,
demonstrating
the
feasibility
of
the
SPE‐
modification strategy to improve the interfacial stability of high‐
voltage cathodes in sulfide‐SEs.

In‐Situ Surface Coating of NCM811
According to our strategy, a precondition to realize in‐situ sur-
face modification is that a certain number of alkaline residues
must exist on the surface of NCM particles to trigger the ring‐
opening polymerization of cyclic organic molecules. As shown in
Figure S1, the supernatant water shows a pH value of 13–14
when NCM811 powder is immersed in the deionized water,
suggesting that there exist a large number of nucleophilic
reaction sites on the NCM811 surface. The selection of DOL and
MA as a mixed precursor in this study for building a polymeric
surface coating was mainly based on the consideration that the
SPE layers derived from DOL have been already reported to have
a high ionic conductivity exceeding 0.1 mS cm–1 [42–45] and that
MA is capable of acting as a copolymerized segment to improve
the electrochemical and structural stability of poly(DOL) SPE
[46–48]. Figure 1A shows the preparation process of SPE‐
NCM811 through the nucleophilic reaction of DOL/MA on
NCM811 particles. As illustrated in Figure 1A, the surface
alkaline groups of NCM811 nucleophilically attack the electron‐
deficient carbon atoms of DOL/MA to initiate in‐situ ring‐
opening polymerization, transforming the liquid precursors to an
SPE layer. According to the possible reaction pathways proposed
and shown in Figure S2, the thus‐formed SPE‐coating layer
should
consist
dominantly
of
the
poly(ether‐ester)
block
copolymer and its derivatives, wherein the polyester fragments
introduced from MA contribute to structural and electrochemical
stability, while the polyether chains derived from DOL serve as a
backbone to promote Li+ transport. To balance the properties of
SPE coating in ionic conductivity, electrochemical stability, and
mechanical durability, the MA concentration in the precursor
solution was optimized. Figure S3 displays that treatment of the
NCM cathode with a 1 M MA/DOL solution enables the elec-
trode to achieve the highest discharge specific capacity and
Coulombic efficiency.

The LPSCl SE used in this study was measured to have a Li+
conductivity of 4.75 mS cm–1 (Figure S10). To ensure an effective
comparison, the bare‐ and SPE‐NCM811 cathodes have the

(CV)

bare‐NCM811 exhibits gradually increased voltage gaps and
decreased current intensities during cycles. These CV features

NCM811 particles from contact with strongly reductive sulfide‐SE.

Both

SPE‐NCM811 cathode at various C‐rates. This cathode delivered
discharge capacities of 199.0 mA h g–1 at 0.2 C, 178.2 mA h g–1
at 0.5 C, and 159.2 mA h g–1 at 1.0 C (1.0 C = 0.366 mA cm–2).
With the current density increasing to 2.0, 5.0, and 10 C, the

contrast, the bare‐NCM811 cathode exhibited considerably

at 1.0 C, and 15.6 mA h g–1 at 10 C (Figure S11). Moreover,
Figure 3D depicts that the SPE‐NCM811 cathode also exhibited
long‐term cycle stability over 11000 cycles at 10 C with a
capacity retention of 71.95%, reflecting excellent interfacial
stability. This interfacial stability was also demonstrated by the
high initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) and stable cycling per-
formance of the SPE‐NCM811 cathode at a low current density
of 100 μA. As can be seen from Figure S12, the ICE of the SPE‐
NCM811 cathode (84.68%) at 0.1 C is substantially higher than
that of the bare‐NCM811 cathode (68.23%). Figure S13 displays
that at a low current rate of 100 μA, the bare‐NCM811 cathode
showed an accelerated capacity decay with a capacity retention
of only 33.4% after 50 cycles, suggesting a severe self‐discharge
(parasitic reaction) occurring at the NCM811/Li6PS5Cl inter-
face. In contrast, the SPE‐NCM811 cathode demonstrated a
superior capacity retention of 95.1% in the same case, suggest-
ing that the self‐discharge reaction is effectively inhibited by the
SPE coating, which acts as a physical isolating barrier to block
the direct contact between NCM811 and Li6PS5Cl.

5.59 × 10–14 cm2 s–1 for bare‐NCM811 (Figure 3F), respectively.

surprising simply because, as an interphase, the SPE‐coating

Interfacial Mechanical Stability of
SPE‐NCM811 in ASSLB
As is well‐known, practical ASSLBs should be built with intimately
contacted
and
structurally
stabilized
solid/solid
(electrode/-
electrolyte) interface even subjected to huge volume changes of
cathode/anode electrodes during cycling [67–69]. Apparently, the
SPE‐NCM811 is favorable to construct such an interface with SEs
because the viscoelastic and deformable SPE‐coating can possibly
act as a buffer layer to bind NCM811/Li6PS5Cl together, forming a
mechanical interlocking interface during cold pressing of cathode
preparation. Therefore, the mechanical stress caused by the volume
change of the NCM811 cathode can be alleviated effectively, and the
interfacial mechanical stability can be well‐maintained during cy-
cling. To confirm this possibility, we characterized the mechanical
properties of bare‐, LNO‐, and SPE‐NCM811 using the atomic force
microscopy (AFM)‐based peak‐force quantitative nanomechanical
mapping (PFQNM) technique. Figure 6A–C compares the AFM
topographies and dynamic mechanical thermal (DMT) modulus 3D
images of LNO‐/bare‐/SPE‐NCM pellets and the line‐cut profile
curves extracted from DMT modulus images, respectively. As can
be seen, the SPE‐NCM pellet has a low Young's modulus with a
mean value of only 4.6 GPa. However, the LNO‐NCM and bare‐
NCM pellets exhibit high Young's modulus of 87.7 and 25.6 GPa,
respectively, within the same height and width distance (Figures 6C
and S20). This comparison demonstrates that the SPE coating has
good flexibility indeed and is therefore favorable for creating an
intimate contact for NCM811 and sulfide‐SE to form a mechanically
stable electrode/electrolyte interface.
The interfacial mechanical stability of the SPE‐NCM811 cathode
was also experimentally evidenced by the focused ion beam‐
scanning electron microscope (FIB‐SEM). Figure 6D–F compares
the FIB‐SEM images of bare‐/LNO‐/SPE‐NCM811 cathodes
before and after cycling. As can be seen, these three cathodes
exhibit a virtually identical morphology without any void and
crack before cycling. After cycling, quite a lot of cracks and voids
appear in the bare‐NCM811 and LNO‐NCM811 cathodes, with
the void fraction of 18% and 8% measured by ImageJ software,
respectively (Figure S21). In contrast, the cycled SPE‐NCM811
cathode still remains intact in morphology and structure, with a
void fraction of only 2% at the solid–solid interface, demon-
strating excellent interfacial mechanical stability. This compari-
son suggests that the SPE coating can effectively buffer the stress
change of the cathode and keep the solid/solid interface struc-
turally stable. Since the mechanical stress‐induced structural
degradation is a severe cause for the electrochemical failure of
the electrodes in ASSLBs, such a mechanically stable interface,
given by the SPE coating, undoubtedly greatly enhances the cy-
cling stability of the NCM811 cathode in ASSLBs.

Conclusions
In summary, we developed an SPE‐coating strategy to coat a
polymeric Li+‐conductive layer on the NCM811 surface by in‐situ
nucleophilic reaction of DOL/MA co‐precursor with the alkaline
sites on the cathode material. Due to the in‐situ ring‐opening
polymerization occurring on the surface alkaline sites, the resulting
SPE‐coating layer is homogeneous, compact, conformal, and
therefore capable of serving as a protection layer to prevent the
contact of high voltage cathode with chemically unstable sulfide‐SE
and thereby to be used for constructing structurally and electro-
chemically stable interphase for ASSLBs. To prove the advantageous
effect of SPE‐coating, we assembled ASSLBs based on SPE‐NCM811
cathode, Li6PS5Cl SE, and LiIn anode and evaluated the electro-
chemical performance and cycling stability of these ASSLBs. The
SPE‐NCM811 cathode in ASSLBs exhibits high capacities of
193.5 mA h g–1 at 0.2 C, 160.9 mA h g–1 at 2.0 C, and 112.3 mA h g–1
at 10 C, particularly, excellent long‐term cycling stabilities over
11000 cycles with a 71.95% capacity retention at 10 C at 25°C, and a
remained capacity of 117.9 mA h g–1 after 8000 cycles at 30 C at
60°C, confirming the possibility of SPE‐coating for improving the
interfacial stability in sulfide‐based ASSLBs.

Phosphate and Borate-Based Composite Interface of
Single-Crystal LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 Enables Excellent

Fu Long, Yuyang Liu, Guobin Zhu *

Abstract: The application of nickel-rich cathodes in lithium-ion batteries has been hampered by its
rapid capacity/voltage fading and limited performance of rate. In this work, a passivation technique
is used to create a stable composite interface on single-crystal LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) surface,
which greatly improves the cycle life-span and high-voltage constancy of cathode with 4.5 and 4.6 V
cut-off voltage. The improved Li+ conductivity of the interface enables a ﬁrm cathode–electrolyte
interphase (CEI), which reduces interfacial side reactions, lowers the risk of safety hazards, and
improves irreversible phase transitions. As a result, the electrochemical performance of single-crystal
Ni-rich cathode are remarkably enhanced. The speciﬁc capacity of 152 mAh g−1 can be delivered at a
charging/discharging rate of 5 C under 4.5 V cut-off voltage, much higher than 115 mAh g−1 of the
pristine NCM811. After 200 cycles at 1 C, the composite interface modiﬁed NCM811 demonstrates
outstanding capacity retention of 85.4% and 83.8% at 4.5 V and 4.6 V cut-off voltage, respectively.
Keywords: lithium-ion batteries; cathode; LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2; boric acid
1. Introduction
In recent years, nickel-rich layered oxide NCM811 has been intensively investigated
as a cathode material for large-scale applications in electric vehicles because of its unique
strengths in terms of theoretical speciﬁc capacity, operating potential, and fabrication
cost [1–4]. Generally, much interest has been generated in the secondary spheres structure
of polycrystalline NCM811 cathode due to its high-level ﬁrst Coulombic efﬁciency for
de-embedded Li+ storage. However, during battery operation, polycrystalline particles
are susceptible to cracking due to inherent anisotropic volume changes, which accelerates
the bulk structure degradation and aggravates interface side reactions, thus shortening the
battery life [5–7].
Compared with the polycrystalline aggregates, the single-crystal NCM811 materials
with micro-sized particles exhibit excellent electrochemical performance because of the
integrated bulk structure and fewer grain boundaries, reducing the interface reaction
caused by intergranular crack [8–11]. However, there are certain downsides to single-
crystalline cathodes. Firstly, because of the smaller primary particles, the single-crystal
NCM811 material has higher irreversible lithium consumption due to their longer Li+
diffusion path [12–14]. Additionally, surface residual lithium compounds (RLCs) emerging
from the manufacturing and storage processes is a major and unavoidable fundamental
ﬂaw. Speciﬁcally, LiOH not only causes cathode slurry gelation, but it also combines with
LiPF6 in the electrolyte to generate HF, which corrodes the electrode and causes transition
metal ion dissolution. The Li2CO3 is also a detrimental substance since it decomposes when
charged to 4.2 V, causing the battery to bulge and shortening its life [15–18]. Additionally,
cationic mixing is caused by the low-valence metal ions moving to the Li+ layer and
occupying the location of Li+. Since a similar ionic radius exists between the Li+ (0.76 Å)
and Ni2+ (0.69 Å), the Ni2+ are prone to occupying the position of Li+ resulting in a barrier

against subsequent Li+ transmission and making the irreversible Li+ consumption increase,
accompanying with transition of crystal structure from layered over spinel to NiO-like rock
salt phase [19–21]. What is worse, apart from the synthesis process, the Li/Ni mixing are
closely related to the severe operating conditions [22–24] (such as extreme charging voltage,
higher working temperature). Oxygen evolution occurs in all nickel-rich cathodes, and
the potential of 4.2 V (vs. Li/Li+ for NCM811) is the onset of oxygen evolution, which is
intimately related with the onset of H2-H3 phase transition and drastically increased as the
cut off potential and temperature rising. The unfavorable triple phase transition is caused
by the Li/Ni mixing and oxygen evolution reaction, which results in oxygen loss from the
cathode materials and thus poses a safety problem. Even worse, this irreversible phase
transition is aggravated when elevating the cut-off potential of cathodes and operating
temperature [25–27].
For the interface and structure problem of high-nickel materials, some artiﬁcial par-
ticular protective coating material, such as metal oxides [28,29], phosphates [30,31], ﬂuo-
rides [32,33], borates [34,35], or lithium-containing binary metal oxides [36,37], are regarded
as one of the most feasible strategies to combat these issues. Among the aforementioned
substances, phosphate coatings exhibit advantages not only due to their brilliant structure
stability which is caused by the strong bond of P-O but also for the special chemical activity.
More speciﬁcally, Li3PO4 coating possesses high ionic conductivity [38], LiH2PO4 coating
can react with Li2CO3, LiOH and the surface lithium impurities, AlPO4 coating appears to
have superior thermal stability and constructs a robust interfacial layer, isolating the erosion
of organic electrolyte to the matrix particles [39,40]. For example, Zhang et al. [40] propose
an advanced surface improvement strategy for NCM811 with fabricating the Li3PO4 nano
layer, which substantially enhances the electrochemical performance of NCM811 due to
the derived fast Li+ diffusion CEI. As surveyed by Hu et al. [41], the coated substance of
LiH2PO4 on the NCM811 surface can react with Li2CO3 and LiOH to form a homogenous
passivation layer of Li4P2O7 with an average of about 35 nm in thickness while sintering,
which effectively alleviate the side reaction. Liu et al. [29] introduced NaH2PO2 to construct
an ultrathin and stable P-doped functional passivation layer, which not only enhances
the stability of the cathode/electrolyte interface, but also inhibits the irreversible phase
transformation and the generation of intergranular cracking. Cho et al. [38] coated AlPO4
on NCM811 via a wet-coating process to attain a decorated nano layer with 10 nm thickness.
As a result, due to the highly covalent bonding property between Al and (PO4)3−, the
rate performance and capacity retention are substantially improved, which provides the
coating with resistance to electrolyte reactions. Since these substances can provide stable
interfacial passivation effects, it is necessary to study their synergistic effect, especially at
high cut-off voltage. In other uses than the coating strategy as mentioned above, electrolyte
additives play a crucial role for Ni-rich cathode in passivating the NCM811 cathode and
sustaining its long-term durability, especially in addressing the extremely unstable interface
under high cut-off voltage for Ni-rich cathodes. Boron-containing compounds, due to the
electron-deﬁcient B atom, are prone to interacting with nucleophilic species (such as F−)
and tend to form stale CEI due to the intense B-O bond, which is often utilized as electrolyte
additives, especially at higher cut-off voltage conditions. For instance, Zhao et al. [42]
reported that the superior electrochemical performance of the NCM811 cathode is capable
of reaching a voltage of 4.6 V by utilizing a unique electrolyte system, which contains
ﬂuorinated carboxylate with a lithium diﬂuoro (oxalate) borate and ﬂuorinated carbonate
additive. The stable inorganic electrode/electrolyte interface rich in B−and F−at high
cut-off voltages prevents the collapse of the cathode structure and the continuous dissolu-
tion of transition metal ions as well as reducing electrochemical polarization. Liu et al. [43]
employed a high-voltage bifunctional nitrile–borate molecule, tris(2-cyanoethyl) borate, as
a ﬁlm-forming additive for LiNi0.88Co0.09Mn0.03O2 cathode. The B-rich additives-derived
CEI layer not only protects the cathode from degradation by interfacial side reactions at
4.7 V, but also enlarges the galvanic oxidation window of the electrolyte. Interestingly,

application of boric acid on NCM811 surface not only removes residual lithium, but also
forms a stable interfacial layer containing B-O compounds [35].
Furthermore, we compare three representative modiﬁcation studies of NCM811. Sim
et al. [6] constructed carbon coatings using super P carbon black to enhance the Li+ and
electron transport capacity of the material. The modiﬁed cathode exhibited a capacity
retention of 87.8% after 80 cycles at 0.5 C, while showing excellent rate performance. Zhang

ion diffusivity and lattice stability. The doped cathode exhibited 86.6% capacity retention

the NCM surface and inhibit the corrosion reaction of HF. After 120 cycles, the capacity
retention of the polysiloxane-coated NCM811 reached 91.5% at 1 C.
Considering the superiorly favorable properties of phosphate and boron-containing
materials for high nickel materials, in this work, we designed a multifunctional composite
passivation interface with aluminum triphosphate (ALP) and boric acid (BA) from the
perspective of clearing the surface residual lithium and improving the high cut-off voltage
performance of the single-crystal NCM811. ALP is a widely used environmental protec-
tion coating, which demonstrates the advantages of good thermal stability and corrosion
resistance, and BA has weak acidity, which is usually used to prepare heat-resistant and
corrosion-resistant glass products. By constructing a phosphorus–boron functional interface
layer on the surface of a high-nickel material, the surface residual lithium is reduced, and
the capacity retention of the modiﬁed cathode has a signiﬁcant improvement, especially at
high voltage (4.5 V/4.6 V). In addition, high temperature and high-voltage performance,
the performance of rate, and thermal stability of the modiﬁed electrode are signiﬁcantly
enhanced. Combined with common advantages of interfacial phosphorylation and interfa-
cial borylation, this artiﬁcial functional passivation interface is projected to give signiﬁcant
beneﬁts in terms of cycle stability and thermostability at high operating potentials, making
single-crystal NCM811 an attractive cathode material.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Preparation of Materials
The experimental NCM811 powder was purchased from Dangsheng Ltd., Co., Beijing,
China. The synthesis diagram of modiﬁed NCM811 as depicted in Figure 1, ﬁrstly, the pre-
cursor was obtained by mixing 20 mg ALP (AlH2P3O10, AR, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical
Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) with 2g NCM811 by mechanical milling and then
the obtained mixing powder was calcinated at 500 ◦C with an O2 atmosphere for 6 h in a
tube furnace. Secondly, 10 mg and 20 mg BA (H3BO3, GR, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical
Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were added to the precursor for further mechanical
milling and then calcined in an Ar atmosphere with 6 h at 250 ◦C to get the ﬁnal products
with different BA mass ratios. Accordingly, the bare single-crystal NCM811 and the two
treated ﬁnal samples were designated as SCNCM, SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 (10 mg BA), and
SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-2 (20 mg BA), respectively.

2.2. Preparation and Testing of Electrodes
The anode powder, polyvinylidene ﬂuoride, and conductive carbon black (weight
ratio 88:5:7) was fully mixed in N-methyl pyrrolidone solvent. The cathode slurry was
thoroughly mixed under Ar atmosphere and coated in aluminum foil (12 mm thick). The
cathode pole piece was calendered and cut into slices (13 mm diameter) after drying at 60 ◦C
for 6 h. The original and modiﬁed samples have similar mass loadings of 4.0 ± 0.1 mg cm−2
and the prepared cathode pole piece was dried for 16 h at 120 ◦C under vacuum. The rough
loading of graphite anode materials and NCM811 cathode was around 2.7 and 4.6 mg cm−2
for a full cell.
The electrochemical properties of the cathodes were tested in CR2032 coin-type cells.
The adopted separator between the cathode and the anode was a porous polypropylene
ﬁlm (Celgard 2500) while lithium foil was used as the counter electrode. The electrolyte
was obtained from Capchem Ltd., Co. China, and is prepared by dissolving 1 mol L−1
LiPF6 in a ternary solvent of ethylene carbonate (EC), ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC), and
dimethyl carbonate (DMC) at a weight ratio of 1:1:1. Three formation cycles were applied
between 2.8 and 4.5 and 4.6 V vs. Li/Li+ at a 0.1 C rate (1 C = 200 mA g−1) on a Maccor
S4000 battery cycler (Maccor Instruments, USA) at 25 ◦C (or 55 ◦C).The rate performance
of the electrodes was tested with different charge/discharge rates (0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 C,
respectively). Long-term cycling capability was compared with 1 C charge and discharge
rates. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) information was collected in the
frequency range of 105 to 10−2 Hz with an amplitude of 5 mV on an IM6 electrochemical
workstation (Zahner, Germany) at 25 ◦C at a certain electrode potential. The thermal
stability of charged cathodes was evaluated by differential scanning calorimeter. The
cathodes were ﬁrst charged to 4.5 V at 0.1 C and then held at 4.5 V for an additional 1 h.
The cathode materials were collected from the dismantled cells and then washed with
DMC to remove remaining electrolytes and dried. Charged cathode powders (5.0 mg) were

2.3. Material Characterization
The crystalline phase of the NCM811 material was determined by using X-ray diffrac-

3. Results and Discussion
The SEM images of the original SCNCM and SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 samples are shown
in Figure 2a,b. The diameter of the original SCNCM particles is about 2–3 µm and it has
a smooth and clean surface. However, homogeneous coating can be clearly observed in
SEM and TEM images (Figure 2c) of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample, suggesting a composite
interfacial layer has been formed by bonding reaction with residual lithium on the surface
of NCM811 particles. In addition, the spatial elements distribution of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1
sample was obtained by the SEM-Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and the
elements (B, Al, and P) were equally distributed over the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample
(Figure 2d–f). This indicates that the ultrathin uniform passivation layer is produced
successfully on the SCNCM surface. Moreover, the crystallographic structures of sample
were determined by XRD tests and presented in Figure 2g,h. All XRD patterns correspond
to a well-deﬁned α-NaFeO2 rhombohedral structure (space group of R-3m) [44]. The
splitting of (108)/(110) and (006)/(102) peaks in the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 veriﬁes the
ordered layered structure and indicates that the fundamental structure of the SCNCM
is not damaged during the modiﬁed process. The degree of Li/Ni cation mixing can be
reﬂected by calculating the intensity ratio of the peak (003) to peak (104), and a higher
value of I (003)/I (104) implies a more sequential layer structure. The value of I (003)/I (104)
is 2.01 for the SCNCM sample and 2.41 for the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample, signifying
the Li/Ni mixing in the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample is beneﬁcially reduced [20]. This
may be attributed to the surface reconstruction during chemical conversion and Al3+ enter
into the Ni site under high temperature re-treatment process. For the pristine SCNCM,
SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1, and SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-2, the BET surface area is obtained to be
1.42, 1.39, and 1.44 m2 g−1, respectively.
The surface element sensitive characterization method XPS was used to evaluate the
samples in order to furthermore investigate the compound composition of the coating. The
XPS ﬁndings of the SCNCM and SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 samples are shown in Figure 3, and
the accuracy of the calibration spectrum depends on a C1s peak of 284.8 eV. As shown in
Figure 3a,e, the C1s peak with reduced intensity demonstrates that the interfacial reactions
between the ALP/B and RLCs since the Li2CO3 content originating from the surface
residual lithium compounds is decreased. In addition, the characteristic B, Al, and P peaks
are only detected on the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample as expected. Speciﬁcally, as shown
in Figure 3f–h, binding energy at 191.8 eV for the B-O bond originated from the borates and
the binding energy of 133.3 eV represents a metal phosphate, which indicates the formation
of a phosphorus boronated bi-functional passivation layer with AlPO4, Li3PO4 and lithium
boron oxide (Li3BO3, B2O3).

The schematic diagram of the construction process of the functional passivation coating
on the single-crystal NCM811 surface appears in Figure 4. The function-speciﬁc ALP and
BA substances not only possess the chemical activity to react with the residual lithium
compounds (LiOH and Li2CO3), but the resulting by-products are also conducive to the
construction of stable CEI, which can be an efﬁcient solution to the interfacial problem
of the NCM811 cathode. On the one hand, the stable target product of AlPO4 and B2O3
can serve as a solid barrier to safeguard the matrix particles from harmful substances
produced by electrolyte decomposition at higher operation voltage (4.5 V/4.6 V) and the
boron-containing compounds can interact with the HF resulting from the decomposition

7 of 15
of lithium salt in the electrolyte to form B-F compounds due to the ﬂuorophilic property
of boron, which signiﬁcantly reduces degradation of the active material by HF, inhibits
the dissolution of transition metals, alleviates the side reactions in interface areas, delays
the serious structural degradation of high-nickel materials, and prolongs the life of a cell
under high-voltage working conditions. On the other hand, not only the crystalline Li3PO4
can be selected as a channel to facilitate the transfer of Li+ but the Li3BO3 with high ionic
conductivity can also effectively promote the diffusion of Li+, resulting in a low impedance
phosphate–boron interface layer being formed. It is because of the synergy of the two
functional species that the battery exhibits an excellent electrochemical property.

We tested the electrochemical performance of various electrode materials under error
to verify the positive effect of the composite grafted ALP/BA layer on NCM811. The
charge/discharge patterns in the ﬁrst cycle between 2.8 and 4.5 V at 0.1 C and 25 ◦C are dis-
played in Figure 5a. The ﬁrst charge/discharge-speciﬁc capacities of the different electrodes
are 244/209, 251/215, and 245/208 mAh g−1 and the calculated initial Coulombic efﬁciency
(ICE) is 85.8, 85.7, and 84.9%, respectively. Compared with the pristine cathode, the modi-
ﬁed sample of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 delivers a higher speciﬁc capacity while maintaining
a similar ICE, which indicates a more optimal Li+ deintercalation–intercalation process.
However, for the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-2 sample, it exhibits relatively low ICE and discharge
speciﬁc capacity, indicating that this is not the optimal modiﬁcation conditions, which
should be caused by an excess of boric acid. Furthermore, Figure 5b characterizes the cycling
stability of all electrodes at room temperature over a long period of time. After 200 cycles
under 1C, the modiﬁed electrode of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 has a reversible speciﬁc capacity
of 158 mAh g−1 and a capacity retention up to 85.4%. The reversible speciﬁc capacity of
the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-2 was 122 mAh g−1 with a capacity retention of 66.4%, whereas
the matching results for the bare SCNCM electrode are signiﬁcantly lower (111 mAh g−1
and 60.2%), denoting the cycling stability of the modiﬁed SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrode
has been dramatically improved. To emphasize the remarkable performance of ALP/BA
layer, we further increased the cut-off voltage of the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrode to
4.6 V and also tested the higher temperature and higher voltage performance (4.5 V, 55 ◦C).
The electrochemical performance of the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrode with 4.6 V is pre-
sented in Figure 5c,d. The ﬁrst charge/discharge speciﬁc capacities of the SCNCM and
SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrodes were 251/216 and 247/218 mAh g−1, the corresponding
ICE was 86.0% and 88.1%, respectively. In addition to the enhanced ICE, the capacity re-
tention rate of the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrode (83.8%) is much greater than that of the
primitive electrode (55.1%). It is easy to ﬁnd that as the cut-off voltage rises, the decaying

the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrode exerts a higher speciﬁc capacity (209 mAh g−1) and
the recovered speciﬁc capacity tends to be more stable. Under the harsh conditions of
4.5 V cut-off voltage, the ALP/BA decorated NCM811 performs outstandingly both in the
long-term cycling and capacity retention at high circulation rate [47,48]. Such great enhance-
ment of electrochemical properties implies that the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample exhibits
an acceleration of the electrode reactivity, which is attributed to the CEI formed by the
participation of ALP/BA. To explore the electrode kinetics, the Nyquist plots of the SCNCM
and SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 cathodes after cycling 200 at 4.5 V are shown in Figure 6b. The
Nyquist plots consist of the electrolyte resistance (Re), the cathode/electrolyte interphase
impedance (RCEI) in the high-frequency region corresponds to the Li+ migration through
the surface insulating layer of active material particles, the charge-transfer resistance (RCT)
in the medium-frequency region reﬂects the charge transfer between electrons and ions
at the conductive junction, and the Warburg impedance in the low-frequency region rep-
resents the solid diffusion process of Li+ in the active material particles. Remarkably, the
values of RCEI and RCT for the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 electrode (58.5 and 79.4 Ω) are smaller
than the reference SCNCM (64.4 and 146.6 Ω), especially for the RCT value, which leads to
the conclusion of a signiﬁcant reduction in the charge transfer resistance on the particle
surface. As a result, due to the involvement of the coating substance, a robust and lower
impedance CEI is generated during electrochemical cycling. Furthermore, as shown in
Figure S1, we came to a similar conclusion by analyzing the Nyquist plots of electrodes
at a cut-off voltage of 4.6 V, compared with pristine electrode (319.4 Ω), the RCT values
for the treated electrode (219.4 Ω) was signiﬁcantly lower. Furthermore, the galvanostatic
intermittent titration technique (GITT) can more sufﬁciently explain the improvement in
rate cycle stability, and the calculation results of the Li+ diffusion coefﬁcient are shown in
Figure 6c. The Li+ diffusion coefﬁcient of the modiﬁed electrode is higher than the pristine
electrode at 4.5 V cut-off voltages during a charge/discharge process, this indicates that the
modiﬁed samples possess faster Li+ transport. The improvement in Li+ transport rate can
be attributed to the surface conductive lithium salt, which is formed by the reaction of ALP
and BA with residual lithium compounds. In order to evaluate the thermal performance
of the cathode materials, DSC measurements were performed as the electrode charged to
4.5 V. As Figure 6d shows, the exothermic reaction of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 occurred at a
higher temperature of 246.38 ◦C with a heat generation of 474.95 J g−1, compared with the
data of 237.04 ◦C and 565.06 J g−1 for the reference sample, respectively. The exothermic
reaction is affected by the release of oxygen during the decomposition of the high-nickel
cathode materials. These outstanding thermal behaviors of the modiﬁed cathode materials
may be related to the phosphate compounds and borate compounds that are very stable
against both the chemical and thermal reactions owing to the strong PO43−and BO33−
bonding, which make the decrease of cathode surface exposed to the electrolyte to reduce
the generation of oxygen due to a side interface reaction so that the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1
is able to reduce the amount of heat release and delay the thermal peak.
The differential capacity versus electrode potential (dQ/dV) curves were calculated
by differentiating the 10th and 100th charge–discharge curves to reveal the phase transition
behavior during deep electrochemical cycles. By analyzing the differential capacity curves
at different cut-off voltages (Figure 7a–d), three different redox peaks can be observed
in the previous curves, representing the phase transition from H1 to M, from M to H2,
and from H2 to H3, respectively. However, after 100 cycles, the peaks representing the
H2-to-H3 phase transition have completely disappeared for the pristine electrode, and the
peaks are clearly observed in the curves of the modiﬁed electrode, which suggests that
this irreversible phase transition of the material from layered to spinel and deleterious
rock salt structures is signiﬁcantly suppressed during multiple electrochemical cycles.
This irreversible phase transition behavior becomes more severe as the cut-off voltage is
increased to 4.6 V. For the SCNCM electrode, the peaks representing the M-to-H2 and H2-
to-H3 phase transitions have completely disappeared after 100 cycles, however, these two
characteristic peaks are observed in the SCNCM electrode. Furthermore, the anodic peaks of

To further explore the factors of capacity degradation, a comparative analysis of the
electrode surfaces of SCNCM and SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 cathodes was analyzed relying on
XPS. The XPS ﬁtting results of the electrodes after 200 cycles at 4.5 V cut-off voltage in the
region of C 1s, F 1s, and Ni 2p are displayed in Figure 8. As shown in Figure 8a,d, the B 1s
signals are detected in the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample and the B-O bond (192.4 eV) and B-
F bond (194.9 eV) peaks appear in the B1s spectrum. A plausible explanation is that borates
are participating in the formation of CEI during the electrochemical cycling, which forms a
protective barrier against the corrosion of HF by forming B-F or B-O-based compounds.
Accordingly, the presence of B-rich CEI on the surface of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 inhibits the
dissolution of transition metal ions, interfacial side reactions of the electrode/electrolyte and
irreversible phase transition. Furthermore, P 2p spectra (Figure 8b,e) reveal a critical point,
the relative intensity of the peak corresponding to LixPFy (137.5 eV) is stronger in SCNCM,
indicating a more severe decomposition behavior of the lithium salt (LiPF6). Additionally,
from Figure 8c,f, the F1s peak in the SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 sample shows mainly two
components LiF (685.4 eV) and LixPFy (687.6 eV), both of which are produced by the
decomposition of the lithium salt on the electrode surface. The signiﬁcantly weaker intensity
of the F1s peak of SCNCM/(ALP+BA)-1 compared to SCNCM indicates a milder side effect

4. Conclusions
In this study, in order to strengthen the performance of single-crystal NCM811 cathode
at high operation voltage, we designed a multifunctional composite passivation interface
with ALP and BA from the perspective of clearing the surface residual lithium and enhanc-
ing the high-voltage performance of the single-crystal NCM811 cathode. Speciﬁcally, the
stable by-products AlPO4 and B2O3 serve as a solid barrier to shield the substrate from
the attack of hazardous substances created by electrolyte decomposition at high operation
voltage, while the crystalline Li3PO4 and L3BO3 act as a selective channel facilitating the
transfer of Li+. As a result, the cycling performance of the cathode is notably improved
at 4.5 V and 4.6 V. Under 4.5 V cut-off voltage, a speciﬁc capacity of 152 mAh g−1 can be
delivered at a charging/discharging rate of 5 C, while the original cathode is signiﬁcantly
lower exhibiting only 115 mAh g−1. Even at high cutoff voltages of 4.5 V and 4.6 V, the
modiﬁed samples performed excellent capacity retention of 85.4% and 83.8% after 200 cy-
cles at 1 C, respectively. In addition, the harsh high-voltage-temperature (4.5 V, 55 ◦C)
performance of the modiﬁed cathode increased from 54.8% to 80.3% after 100 cycles and
the improvement of heat resistance was realized. This functional passivation interface
is projected to give signiﬁcant beneﬁts in both performance of rate and cycle stability at
high operating potentials, making single-crystal NCM811 an attractive cathode material for
high-energy LIB.
