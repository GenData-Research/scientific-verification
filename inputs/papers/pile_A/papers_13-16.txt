Atomic and Molecular Structure Regulated 
In Situ Cross‑Linked Polyurethane Gel Electrolyte 
for High‑Performance Lithium Metal Batteries

HIGHLIGHTS
•	 Hybrid polyester/polyether segments simultaneously enable high-voltage stability (4.97 V) and fast ­Li+ desolvation in Li||NCM811 
batteries for both cathode and anode compatibility.
•	 The sp2-hybridized boron Lewis acid centers anchor anions via acid-base interactions, boosting ­Li+ transference number to 0.78 and 
enhancing rate capability (157.7 mAh ­g−1 at 2 C).
•	 Hydrogen bonding restructures solvation sheath, promoting anion-derived inorganic-rich solid electrolyte interphase/cathode elec-
trolyte interphase that delivers 81.7% capacity retention after 500 cycles.
ABSTRACT  The incompatibility of conventional electrolytes with high-voltage 
cathodes and lithium metal anodes limits the performance of lithium metal batter-
ies (LMBs). Here, an in situ cross-linked polyurethane gel electrolyte (G-P3 AR) is 
designed through atomic and molecular structure regulation. The polyester segments 
widen the highest occupied molecular orbital–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital 
gap, extending the electrochemical stability window to 4.97 V for compatibility 
with NCM811 cathodes. Polyether segments exhibit a lower ­Li+ binding energy, 
reducing the desolvation barrier and enhancing anode stability. At the atomic level, 
sp2-hybridized boron in the chain extender immobilizes anions ­(TFSI− and ­DFOB−) 
through Lewis acid–base interactions, raising the ­Li+ transference number to 0.78 
and enabling exceptional rate capability (157.7 mAh ­g−1 at 2 C in the Li||NCM811 
cell). Hydrogen bonding between the polymer and solvent restructures the solvation 
sheath, promoting inorganic-rich interphases. The Li|G-P3 AR|NCM811 cell retains 
81.7% capacity after 500 cycles at 0.5 C charge/1 C discharge, demonstrating a rational electrolyte design strategy for high-performance 
LMBs.
KEYWORDS  In situ polymerization; Lithium metal batteries; Polyurethane electrolytes; Solvation structure

1  Introduction
The escalating global demand for high-energy–density stor-
age systems, propelled by the rapid adoption of clean energy 
technologies and the explosive growth of the electric vehicle 
market, has starkly revealed the limitations of conventional 
lithium-ion batteries [1, 2]. The ubiquitous “rocking-chair” 
systems, typically based on ­LiCoO2 or ­LiFePO4 cathodes 
paired with graphite anodes, are approaching their theo-
retical energy density ceiling [3, 4]. This inherent limita-
tion has catalyzed intense research into next-generation 
battery chemistries, among which lithium metal batteries 
(LMBs) stand out as a particularly promising candidate. 
The appeal of LMBs lies in the use of metallic lithium as 
the anode, which boasts an ultrahigh theoretical capacity 
(3860 mAh ­g−1) and the lowest electrochemical poten-
tial (− 3.04 V vs. SHE) among all anode materials [5–7]. 
A pivotal strategy to realize the full potential of LMBs is 
to couple the high-capacity lithium metal anodes (LMAs) 
with high-voltage Ni-rich layered oxide cathodes, such as 
­LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) [8, 9]. However, the practi-
cal implementation of this promising Li||NCM811 configu-
ration is severely hampered by the intrinsic inadequacies 
of conventional carbonate-based liquid electrolytes, which 
fail to simultaneously maintain good compatibilities with 
both the highly reductive LMAs and the oxidizing NCM811 
cathodes at high voltage [10, 11].
The core of the electrochemical stability for the 
Li||NCM811 LMBs is a fundamental trade-off in electrolyte 
chemistry. Ether-based electrolytes, known for their good 
compatibility with LMA and ability to form a relatively 
stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), exhibit a narrow 
electrochemical stability window (ESW, typically below 4 V 
vs. Li/Li+) [12]. This leads to their irreversible oxidative 
decomposition at the operating voltages of Ni-rich cath-
odes (≥ 4.3 V) with a thick cathode electrolyte interphase 
(CEI), resulting in rapid capacity fade [13]. Regarding the 
ester-based electrolytes (e.g., carbonates), they usually offer 
a wider ESW, making them ostensibly suitable for high-
voltage operation. However, their strong solvation with ­Li+, 
driven by the highly electronegative carbonyl groups (C=O), 
creates a high desolvation energy barrier at the anode inter-
face, hindering ­Li+ transport kinetics [14, 15]. Furthermore, 
the decomposition of these strongly coordinated solvent mol-
ecules tends to form an SEI rich in organic components (e.g., 
­ROCO2Li), which is mechanically fragile and incapable of 
uniformly regulating ­Li+ deposition or suppressing lithium 
dendrite growth [16]. Researchers have explored various 
strategies to overcome these challenges, such as fluorinated 
ether solvents [17, 18] and high-concentration electrolytes 
(HCEs) [19, 20], but these often introduce new problems 
like high viscosity [21], poor wettability and low ­Li+ trans-
ference number (tLi+) [22], underscoring the need for a more 
holistic and systematic electrolyte design paradigm.
In recent years, in situ polymerized gel polymer electro-
lytes (GPEs) have emerged as a transformative approach 
to break these trade-offs [23]. By forming a solid polymer 
network in situ within the liquid electrolyte, GPEs combine 
the high ionic conductivity of liquids with the enhanced 
mechanical stability and interfacial compatibility of sol-
ids [24–26]. The polymer network can effectively regulate 
solvent mobility [27, 28] and influence ion coordination 
environments [29, 30]. Recent advances in this field have 
explored diverse strategies for tailoring solvation structures, 
ranging from covalently integrating anions into polymer 
backbones [31] to employing fluorinated monomers that cre-
ate weakly solvating domains [32]. While these approaches 
have demonstrated impressive performance, they often rely 
on bespoke monomer design, which may complicate synthe-
sis and scalability. Alternatively, leveraging commercially 
available polymers offers a complementary and more acces-
sible pathway to achieve comparable solvation regulation. 
Among various polymers, polyurethanes have garnered 
significant attention as a particularly versatile matrix for 
advanced electrolytes. As a class of block copolymers, pol-
yurethanes possess a unique microphase-separated structure 
comprising “soft” segments (often polyethers or polyesters) 
that facilitate ion transport and “hard” segments that pro-
vide mechanical integrity [9, 33, 34]. Crucially, the chemical 
composition and ratio of these segments can be precisely 
tailored, allowing for multi-dimensional customization of 
properties such as the electronic structure, functional group 
density, intermolecular forces and the spatial configuration 
of the cross-linked network. This inherent structural tunabil-
ity positions polyurethane as an ideal matrix for designing 
electrolytes that can simultaneously address the multifaceted 
challenges of LMBs. However, existing polyurethane-based 
electrolytes predominantly rely on single-type soft seg-
ments (either polyether or polyester), inevitably inheriting 
the intrinsic trade-offs of conventional liquid electrolytes

chain at the electrode interface, thereby enhancing anode

2  Experimental Section
2.1  Materials
Ethylene carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), lith-
ium bis (trifluoromethanesulfonyl) imide (LiTFSI, 99.95%) 
and lithium difluoro(oxalato)borate (LiDFOB, 99.95%) 
were purchased from DoDochem Co., Ltd. Trimethylol-
propane ethoxylate (PEG1014, Mw = 1014), polycaprolac-
tone triol (PCL830, Mw = 830), L-lysine diisocyanate (LDI, 
95%), (2,6-difluoro-4-hydroxyphenyl)boronic acid (DHBA, 
97%) and dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL, 95%) were sourced 
from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 
­LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811), aluminum foil (18 μm, 
99.3%) and copper foil (9 μm, 99.3%) were supplied from 
Kejing Star Technology Corp. Super P (99.9%), polyvi-
nylidene difluoride (PVDF), Li foils (15.6 mm diameter 
disk, 99.95%) were purchased from Guangdong Canrd New 
Energy Technology Co., Ltd.
2.2  Preparation of GPEs
Liquid electrolyte (LE) was prepared by dissolving 2 M 
LiTFSI and 0.2 M LiDFOB in a mixture of EC and DMC 
with a volume ratio of 3:7. G-P(EG-U): PEG1014 was dis-
solved in LE under stirring until a clear solution formed. 
LDI was then added at a molar ratio of PEG1014 to LDI of 
2:3, with the combined weight of both components account-
ing for 15 wt% of the total electrolyte mass. After stirring for 
30 min at room temperature, the catalyst (0.5 wt% DBTDL) 
was introduced. The resulting homogeneous solution was 
injected into either coin-type cells (50 µL) or pouch cells 
(3.5 g ­Ah−1) and sealed. In situ polymerization was con-
ducted by heating the cells at 45 °C for 10 h to form GPEs. 
All procedures, including electrolyte preparation and cell 
assembly, were performed in an argon-filled glove box (Mik-
rouna; ­O2 < 0.01 ppm, ­H2O < 0.01 ppm) under ultrapure 
argon (≥ 99.99%, Air Products).
G-P(CL-U): PCL830 was dissolved in LE under stir-
ring until a clear solution formed. LDI was then added at 
a molar ratio of PCL830 to LDI of 2:3, with the combined 
weight of both components accounting for 15 wt% of the 
total electrolyte mass. Subsequent procedures followed the 
same protocol as above.
G-P3: PEG1014 and PCL830 (1:1 molar ratio) were dis-
solved in LE under stirring until a clear solution formed. 
LDI was then added at a molar ratio of PEG 1014 to LDI 
of 1:3. Subsequent procedures followed the same protocol 
as above.
G-P(CL-U) AR: PCL830 was dissolved in LE under stir-
ring until a clear solution formed. LDI was then added at a 
molar ratio of PCL830 to LDI of 1:3, followed by polym-
erization at 45 °C for 2 h after thorough mixing. DHBA was 
then introduced at a PCL830: DHBA molar ratio of 1:1. 
After dissolution via stirring, the catalyst was added to form 
the precursor solution. Subsequent procedures followed the 
same protocol as above.
G-P(EG-U) AR: The preparation procedure was identical 
to that of G-P(CL-U) AR, except that PCL830 was replaced 
by PEG1014.
G-P3 AR: PEG1014 and PCL830 (1:1 molar ratio) were 
dissolved in LE under stirring until a clear solution formed. 
LDI was then added at a molar ratio of PEG1014 to LDI of 
1:6, followed by polymerization at 45 °C for 2 h after thor-
ough mixing. DHBA was then introduced at a PEG1014: 
DHBA molar ratio of 1:2. After dissolution via stirring, the 
catalyst was added to form the precursor solution. Subse-
quent procedures followed the same protocol as above.
2.3  Preparation of P3 and P3 AR
The required reagents were added to DMC according to the 
above procedures. After thermal polymerization, the mixture 
was further heated at 60 °C for 12 h to remove DMC.

3  Results and Discussion
3.1  Molecular Structure Design and Electrochemical 
Properties
Ether-based and ester-based electrolytes exhibit comple-
mentary advantages on LMAs and high-voltage cathodes. 
However, simple physical mixing may lead to crosstalk and 
induce a shortcoming effect [36]. Based on these considera-
tions, we introduced polyether triol (PEG1014) and polyester 
triol (PCL830) into a single polyurethane molecule via a 
simple addition reaction. This design aims to enable G-P3 
AR to achieve a wide ESW along with a relatively weak 
solvating ability. In addition, a sp2-hybridized boron atom 
from DHBA was introduced as an anion receptor, which 
utilizes its electron-deficient nature to coordinate anions and 
accelerate ­Li+ transport. On one hand, this chain extender 
design enables polymer molecules to exhibit lower crystal-
linity. On the other hand, the more abundant hydrogen bond-
ing interactions between polymer and solvent molecules 
reconstruct the solvation structure, inducing inorganic-rich 
SEI/CEI layers on the electrode surface. This unique design 
establishes a dual-scale enhancement mechanism, with the 
characteristic urethane groups of polyurethane serving as the

G-P(EG-U) displays the narrowest ESW, which is in good 
agreement with DFT results. As shown in Fig. 2d, the G-P3 
AR exhibits an extended ESW of up to 4.97 V. The enhance-
ment over the G-P3 (4.93 V) is attributed to the electron-
withdrawing C–F groups in the DHBA, which effectively 
lower the electron density around the oxygen atoms, mak-
ing them less susceptible to oxidation [43, 44]. EIS meas-
urements were performed to determine the tLi+ of different 
electrolytes. Critically, the tLi+ increases from 0.44 for the 
LE (Fig. S7a) and 0.55 for the G-P3 (Fig. S7b) to 0.78 for 
the G-P3 AR (Fig. 2e). This superior enhancement substan-
tially mitigates concentration polarization during cycling, 
resulting in improved rate capability and prolonging cycle 
life in high-voltage applications [45, 46]. Moreover, the 
increased tLi+ directly validates the sp2-hybridized boron in 
DHBA as an efficient anion acceptor.
3.2  Modulating Solvation Structure of Electrolytes
The solvation structure of ­Li+ plays a critical role in deter-
mining ion transport kinetics and interfacial stability. In the 
GPEs, the restructuring of the ­Li+ solvation environment 
arises from the synergistic interplay between direct chelation 
and intermolecular hydrogen bonding. The polymer back-
bone contains multiple Lewis basic sites, including ether 
oxygen atoms in polyether segments and carbonyl oxygen 
atoms in urethane linkages and polyester segments, which 
competitively coordinate with ­Li+ against EC/DMC solvent 
molecules, thereby weakening ­Li+–solvent binding [47, 48]. 
Concurrently, hydrogen bonding interactions between the 
polymer backbone and solvent molecules further modu-
late ­Li+ coordination environments [49, 50]. Specifically, 
the –NH groups in the polymer chains act as hydrogen 
bond donors, while solvent molecules (EC, DMC) serve as 
hydrogen bond acceptors. This interaction redistributes the 
solvent electron cloud, reducing their most negative elec-
trostatic potential (ESPmin), thereby weakening ­Li+–solvent 
coordination and facilitating anion participation in ­Li+ con-
duction. Given that the characteristic –NH–COO– groups 
in polyurethanes serve as both hydrogen bond acceptors 
and donors, extensive interchain hydrogen bonding exists 
within the polymer matrix. The hydrogen bonding ener-
gies between the –NH–COO– group and EC/DMC were 
quantified via DFT calculations (Fig. 3a), revealing favora-
ble interactions between the –NH donor and the carbonyl 
oxygen acceptors of the solvent molecules. This computa-
tional result demonstrates the intrinsic hydrogen-bonding 
donor capacity of –NH toward external carbonyl oxygens. 
Experimentally, FTIR spectroscopy was performed on the 
dry polymer matrices and the corresponding gel electrolytes 
to probe how solvent molecules interact with the hydrogen-
bonded polymer network. In the P3 AR, the N–H stretch-
ing vibration appears at 3336.8 ­cm−1, reflecting extensive

larger for G-P3 AR (56.0 ­cm−1) than for G-P3 (39.1 ­cm−1), 
because DHBA increases the –NH group density at equal 
mass thereby strengthening polymer–solvent interactions 
and disrupting a greater fraction of the original interchain 
hydrogen bonding network [51]. The competitive disrup-
tion of interchain hydrogen bonds by penetrating solvent 
molecules, coupled with the immobilization of solvent car-
bonyls via new hydrogen bonding interactions, attenuates 
the solvating ability of solvent molecules. Furthermore, as 
shown in Fig. 3b, the binding energies between ­Li+ and the 
original solvent molecules versus those regulated by hydro-
gen bonding reveal a significant reduction from − 2.63 to 
− 1.63 eV in the hydrogen-bond-regulated EC and − 1.96 
to − 1.50 eV in the hydrogen-bond-regulated DMC. This 
marked attenuation quantitatively confirms the weakened 
solvation ability of solvent molecules. The interactions opti-
mize electrolyte performance through two key pathways: 
firstly, they reconstruct the solvation structure by reducing 
the binding strength between solvent molecules and ­Li+, 
thereby facilitating the ­Li+ desolvation process; secondly, 
the weakened solvent solvation enables anions to access the 
primary solvation sheath more readily, promoting anion-
derived decomposition reactions that contribute to the for-
mation of inorganic-rich SEI/CEI layers [52, 53]. To quan-
tify the ­Li+ desolvation capability, temperature-dependent 
EIS measurements were performed on Li||Li symmetric cells 
(Fig. 3c). The calculations based on the Arrhenius equation 
show that the ­Li+ desolvation activation energy (Ea, ct) of the 
G-P3 AR is 38.9 kJ ­mol−1, which is significantly lower than 
those of the other systems (LE: Ea, ct = 49.1 kJ ­mol−1; G-P3: 
Ea, ct = 41.7 kJ ­mol−1). These values demonstrate that the 
abundant hydrogen bond donors in the G-P3 AR facilitate 
desolvation and endow ­Li+ with fast transport kinetics.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted to 
characterize ­Li+ migration dynamics in LE and G-P3 AR, 
revealing profound structural rearrangements driven by this 
binding preference. Radial distribution function (RDF) and 
coordination number n(r) analyses quantitatively confirm 
the solvation structure transition in the G-P3 AR: the n(r) 
of ­Li+–O (EC/DMC) drastically decreases due to hydrogen 
bonding, indicating the partial displacement of solvents. The 
suppressed solvent coordination reduces parasitic reduction 
reactions and mitigates solvent decomposition. Concur-
rently, the RDF analysis validates anion-dominated coordi-
nation dynamics, where the surge in ­Li+–O ­(TFSI−/DFOB−) 
peak intensity confirms preferential ­Li+–anion binding over 
solvent interactions (Fig. 3d, e). Moreover, based on the sta-
tistics of the number of ions/solvent molecules coordinated 
to ­Li+, the percentages of solvation structures for the LE and 
G-P3 AR were determined (Fig. 3f). Solvation structures 
with zero anions in the ­Li+ solvation sheath are defined as 
SSIPs, those with one anion as CIPs, and those with two or 
more anions as AGGs [54]. In the G-P3 AR, the propor-
tion of SSIPs (16.6%) is much lower than that in the LE 
(28.2%), replaced by higher proportions of CIPs and AGGs. 
This change significantly promotes anion-derived decompo-
sition, which is critical for forming robust SEI/CEI layers.
As shown in Fig. 3g, h, the introduction of in situ polyure-
thane significantly reduces the SSIPs’ proportion as validated 
by Raman spectroscopy. This indicates that hydrogen bonding 
interactions effectively promote the broader participation of 
anions ­(TFSI− or ­DFOB−) in the ­Li+ solvation sheath, lead-
ing to an increased proportion of CIPs and AGGs. 7Li nuclear 
magnetic resonance (NMR) further validates this solvation 
restructuring (Fig. 3i). Compared to the LE, the G-P3 exhibits 
a downfield shift to − 0.411 ppm, while the G-P3 AR shows 
a more pronounced shift to − 0.340 ppm, indicating reduced 
electron density around ­Li+ due to enhanced anion coordi-
nation [55, 56]. Notably, the G-P3 AR performs better than 
the G-P3, which is attributed to the introduction of the chain 
extender, resulting in more hydrogen bonds within the polymer 
network at the same mass fraction in the GPEs.

G-P3 AR with both LMAs and high-voltage cathodes. More-

electrolyte, cells with high-mass-loading NCM811 cathodes

the LE suffers a short circuit as early as 46 cycles. The G-P3 
system begins to show significant capacity decay after 118 
cycles, with a capacity retention of only 58.6% after 132 
cycles. Additionally, the single-layer Li||NCM811 pouch cell 
with limited lithium supply was assembled using the same 
cathode. As shown in Fig. S14, the Li|G-P3 AR|NCM811 
pouch cell delivers an initial capacity of 193.6 mAh ­g−1 and 
maintains 85.5% capacity retention over 50 cycles at 0.2 C. 
Notably, it can power an LED light and remain stable even 
under bending, puncturing and shearing conditions.
3.4  Interfacial Compatibility
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images reveal that the 
LE and G-P3 induce mossy lithium deposits and dendritic 
structures on the lithium surface (Fig. S15a, b), indicating 
their failure to form a stable SEI capable of suppressing 
interfacial side reactions and ensuring uniform deposi-
tion/dissolution of ­Li+. In contrast, the G-P3 AR enables a 
smooth and dense lithium metal surface (Fig. S15c), attrib-
uted to its low ­Li+ desolvation barrier, which accelerates 
transport kinetics and reduces solvent-induced interfacial 
instability. In-depth X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) 
was used to analyze the composition of SEI. As shown in 
Fig. 5c, F 1s and O 1s spectra provide crucial insights into 
the inorganic-rich nature of the SEI formed by the G-P3 
AR. In the F 1s spectra, the G-P3 AR exhibits a signifi-

LE (Fig. 5a) and G-P3 (Fig. 5b), while the –CF2* signal at 
686.6 eV is substantially attenuated [61, 62], confirming that 
the LiF-rich SEI layer suppresses further decomposition of 
lithium salt [63]. The O 1s spectra reveal the presence of 
­Li2O and ­Li2CO3 in the G-P3 AR system, with prominent

voltage cathodes, the NCM811 cathodes after 25 cycles

components. As shown in Fig. 5j–l, the analysis of F 1s spec-

that of the Li|LE|NCM811 cell, while the surface of the LE-

significantly suppressed intensity provides direct evidence 
for the formation of thicker CEI layer on the LE-cycled 
cathode, which prevents the effective probing of the interior 
NCM811 by XPS. These results indicate that the solvation 
reconstruction effect of the G-P3 AR promotes the formation 
of robust, dense SEI and CEI layers, thereby demonstrating 
the superior interfacial compatibility with both LMAs and 
NCM811 cathodes.
4  Conclusions
Based on the synergistic modulation of atomic and molec-
ular structures, this work successfully develops an in situ 
cross-linked polyurethane gel electrolyte (G-P3 AR) for 
high-performance LMBs. It achieves an extended ESW 
of 4.97 V, a high tLi+ of 0.78, and excellent compatibility 
with both LMAs and high-voltage NCM811 cathodes. The 
rational molecular design incorporates polyester segments to 
enhance oxidation resistance, polyether segments to facilitate 
rapid desolvation, and sp2-hybridized boron atoms to immo-
bilize anions via Lewis acid–base interactions. Furthermore, 
the abundant hydrogen bonding restructures the solvation 
sheath, promoting anion participation and the formation of 
inorganic-rich SEI/CEI layers. As a result, the Li||Li sym-
metric cell demonstrates stable cycling over 1000 h, and 
the Li||NCM811 coin cell retains 81.7% capacity after 500 
cycles at 0.5 C charge/1 C discharge. This work provides a 
scalable and practical electrolyte design paradigm, offering

In this study, we investigate the effect of PO4
3−polyanion incorporation on the physicochemical and electrochemical properties of
Ni-rich layered Li(Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1)O2 (Px-NCM811, where x = 0.0, 0.3, and 0.5) cathode materials. A coprecipitation method with
controlled polyanion injection was employed to ensure homogeneous distribution of PO4
3−within the precursor particles.
Comprehensive structural and morphological analyses conﬁrmed that PO4
3−incorporation led to reduced primary particle size
and the formation of a compact, densely packed microstructure, particularly for the P0.3-NCM811 cathode material. X-ray
diffraction (XRD) and Rietveld reﬁnement analyses revealed lattice expansion along the c-axis, while X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) analysis demonstrated suppressed Ni2+ accumulation and surface stabilization via Li3PO4 formation. Electrochem-
ical evaluation showed that P0.3-NCM811 exhibited superior initial discharge capacity (~227 mA h g−1), Coulombic efﬁciency
(~92.7%), rate capability, and cycling stability, with approximately 86.7% capacity retention after 100 cycles at 1.0 C. Electrochem-
ical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further conﬁrmed lower surface ﬁlm and charge transfer resistances, as well as enhanced Li+
diffusion kinetics in the polyanion-modiﬁed cathodes. Differential capacity analysis indicated improved structural reversibility
during phase transitions for P0.3-NCM811, with reduced polarization and minimal H2 →H3 transition-induced degradation.
These results demonstrate that PO4
3−polyanion incorporation is a promising strategy to stabilize the structure and improve the
electrochemical performance of Ni-rich layered oxide cathodes under high-voltage operation (4.5 V vs. Li/Li+).

1. Introduction
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have become essential energy
storage solutions, supporting applications ranging from por-
table electronics to electric vehicles (EVs) and large-scale
energy storage systems (ESSs) [1, 2, 3]. As global demand
intensiﬁes, particularly for EVs, signiﬁcant research efforts
have been dedicated to enhancing LIB performance metrics,
notably energy density, rate capability, and cycle life [4, 5].
Among these, cathode materials profoundly inﬂuence bat-
tery performance due to their complex structural transfor-
mations and electrochemical instability during lithium-ion
(Li+) insertion/extraction processes [6, 7].
Achieving high energy density requires optimization of
electrode material capacity and operating voltage (E = Ah ×
V, where Ah denotes the electrode capacity and V represents
the operating potential) [8]. Layered oxide cathodes, speciﬁ-
cally Ni-rich Li(NixCoyMn1−x−y)O2 (NCM), derived from
Wiley

LiNiO2 (LNO), have emerged as leading candidates because
of their high speciﬁc capacities and reduced cobalt content,
addressing both performance and economic considerations
[9, 10, 11]. In particular, Li(Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1)O2 (NCM811)
has attracted substantial interest owing to its high capacity,
competitive cost, and potential for achieving higher energy
densities required for advanced EV applications [12].
Despite these merits, NCM811 cathodes suffer from rapid
capacity degradation and limited rate capability, especially
under high potential conditions (~4.5 V vs. Li/Li+), essential
for maximizing energy density. High potential operation
exacerbates intrinsic structural instabilities, including Li/Ni
cation disorder, irreversible layered-to-rock-salt phase trans-
formations, and detrimental interfacial side reactions [13, 14,
15]. Additionally, structural degradation of NCM811 is prom-
inently driven by phase transitions during charge, evolving
sequentially from a hexagonal layered (H1) phase to mono-
clinic (M) and further to hexagonal H2 and H3 phases. Par-
ticularly, the H2 →H3 transition at approximately 4.2 V (vs.
Li/Li+) results in anisotropic lattice contraction along the
c-axis, generating substantial internal stresses and micro-
cracks [16, 17]. Such microcracks accelerate electrolyte inﬁl-
tration, triggering undesirable side reactions and promoting
electrochemically inactive NiO phase formation, thereby dra-
matically compromising cycle life [18, 19].
To mitigate these challenges, numerous advanced strate-
gies have been extensively explored. Surface modiﬁcation
using Li+ conductive coatings such as LiAlO2, LaPO4, LiNbO3,
LiTaO3, and lithium phosphate–based compounds (e.g.,
Li3PO4) has proven effective in stabilizing cathode/electrolyte
interfaces, reducing parasitic reactions, and enhancing interfa-
cial Li+ transport kinetics [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Additionally,
precise morphological engineering via controlled coprecipita-
tion methods, including secondary particle alignment, radial
gradient concentration designs, and nano/microstructural tun-
ing, have been actively investigated to alleviate mechanical
stress and reduce crack propagation during repeated cycling.
Moreover, doping strategies employing both cationic species
(e.g., Ti4+, Al3+, Mg2+, Zr4+, and Nb5+) and anionic species
(e.g., F−and Cl−) have garnered signiﬁcant attention [25, 26,
27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. These doping strategies effectively suppress
cation mixing, enhance structural stability by modulating
metal–oxygen bond strength and lattice parameters, and pro-
mote favorable electronic conﬁgurations and ionic diffusion
pathways [32, 33, 34]. Among the various doping methods,
PO4
3−polyanion incorporation stands out due to its ability
to stabilize structural transitions, reduce interfacial impedance,
and improve Li+ diffusivity, signiﬁcantly extending cycling sta-
bility and rate performance of Ni-rich NCM cathodes [35].
In this work, we present a comprehensive investigation
into the structural and electrochemical enhancement of
NCM811 via strategic incorporation of PO4
3−polyanions
(denoted as Px-NCM811, with x representing the molar per-
centage of PO4
3−incorporation: 0.0, 0.3, and 0.5 mol%). The
primary objective is to reinforce the internal crystal frame-
work of NCM811, suppress cation disorder, and alleviate the
severe structural instabilities at high potentials (~4.5 V vs.
Li/Li+). Additionally, a secondary goal is to deliberately
induce the formation of a Li3PO4 surface layer during syn-
thesis, stabilizing the cathode/electrolyte interface and reduc-
ing interfacial side reactions. Among the studied materials,
Px-NCM811 with x = 0.3 exhibited superior electrochemical
performance, including enhanced speciﬁc capacity, improved
Coulombic efﬁciency, and substantially better cycling stability
under rigorous high potential cycling conditions. To elucidate
the mechanisms behind these performance improvements, we
conducted extensive physicochemical and electrochemical
analyses, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelec-
tron spectroscopy (XPS), electron microscopy, and electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). These investigations
provide clear evidence of the beneﬁcial role of PO4
3−incorpo-
ration in stabilizing the internal lattice structure and mitigating
the detrimental high potential structural transformations in
NCM811 cathodes.
2. Experiment
2.1. Material Synthesis. PO4
3−polyanions were incorporated
into Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1(OH)2 precursors via a coprecipitation
method to synthesize polyanion-modiﬁed NCM811, denoted
as Px-NCM811 (x = 0.0, 0.3, and 0.5, where x represents
the molar ratio of PO4
3−incorporation). A 2.0 M metal sul-
fate solution was prepared by dissolving NiSO4·6H2O,
CoSO4·7H2O, and MnSO4·5H2O in a molar ratio of 8:1:1
in deionized water. Additionally, 2.0 M NH4OH and 4.0 M
NaOH solutions were prepared as the chelating and precipi-
tating agents, respectively. To incorporate PO4
3−into the pre-
cursor structure, Na3PO4·12H2O was dissolved in a NaOH
solution and the concentration of Na3PO4·12H2O was adjusted
to control the PO4
3−content. For instance, for P0.3-NCM811,
the concentration of Na3PO4·12H2O was set to 0.3 mol% rela-
tive to the metal sulfates.
The
metal sulfate
solution,
NH4OH,
NaOH,
and
Na3PO4·12H2O + NaOH solutions were separately and con-
tinuously fed into a continuously stirred tank reactor using
precision peristaltic pumps to ensure uniform incorporation
of PO4
3−throughout the precursor particles from core to
surface. The reactor atmosphere was maintained under inert
N2 gas, the pH was kept at approximately 11, and the reac-
tion temperature was maintained at 55°C. The resulting Px-
NCM811 precursors were collected by ﬁltration, thoroughly
washed with deionized water, and dried at 80°C for 24 h. The
dried precursors were then mixed with LiOH·H2O in a
Li/metal molar ratio of 1.02 and calcined in an O2 atmo-
sphere at 780°C for 12 h to obtain the ﬁnal P0.0-NCM811,
P0.3-NCM811, and P0.5-NCM811 cathode materials.

examined via ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscopy
(FE-SEM, ZEISS Sigma), and the elemental distribution
was assessed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) mapping (ZEISS Sigma). The chemical states of the
constituent elements were characterized by XPS (Thermo
Scientiﬁc K-Alpha+).

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Synthesis and Characterization of Px-NCM811 Cathode
Materials. Li(Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1)O2 (referred to as Px-NCM811,
where x = 0.0, 0.3, and 0.5), incorporating varying amounts of
PO4
3−polyanions, was synthesized through a carefully con-
trolled method that modulates the injection rate of the PO4
3−
polyanion source. In brief, to prepare the Px-NCM811 cath-
ode materials, Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1(OH)2 precursors (Px-NCM811
precursors) were ﬁrst synthesized using a coprecipitation
method (details in Section 2). Unlike conventional methods
that introduce PO4
3−postsynthesis, either by reacting with
surface residual Li to form a Li3PO4 layer or by posttreatment
incorporation, we incorporated the PO4
3−polyanions directly
during the coprecipitation process. As illustrated in Figure 1,
this strategy was deliberately designed to modulate both the
surface chemistry and the bulk structure of NCM811. By pre-
cisely controlling the injection rate, we enabled a uniform dis-
tribution of PO4
3−polyanions from the core to the surface of
the precursors.
As conﬁrmed by SEM images (Figure S1), all Px-NCM811
precursors exhibited needle-like primary particles aggregated
into spherical secondary particles. In addition, EDS mapping
of all Px-NCM811 precursors revealed a homogeneous distri-
bution of Ni, Co, and Mn throughout the particles (Figures
S2–S4). Notably, the P0.3-NCM811 (Figure S3) and P0.5-
NCM811 (Figure S4) precursors also exhibited a uniform
distribution of phosphorus, further conﬁrming the successful
incorporation of PO4
3−during the coprecipitation step. How-
ever, after calcination with a Li source to obtain the ﬁnal Px-
NCM811 cathode materials, SEM images revealed a clear

which may in turn positively inﬂuence the electrochemical

To investigate the structural characteristics of the synthe-
sized Px-NCM811 cathode materials, XRD analysis was per-
formed (Figure 2d–g). All samples exhibited well-deﬁned
layered structures characteristic of Li(Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1)O2,
with a rhombohedral structure belonging to the R−3m space
group. Despite the incorporation of PO4
3−, no secondary
phases or impurity peaks were detected in the XRD patterns
of P0.3-NCM811 and P0.5-NCM811 cathode materials. More-
over, the distinct splitting of the (006)/(102) and (108)/(110)
peaks demonstrates the high crystallinity of the cathode
materials, further supporting the successful incorporation
of PO4
3−polyanions (Figure 2e,f) [36]. To gain more detailed
insight into the structural variations, Rietveld reﬁnement was
conducted (Figure 2h–j), and the results are summarized in
Table S1. The a-axis lattice parameter remained nearly
unchanged across the series; however, a gradual increase in
the c-axis lattice parameter was observed with increasing
PO4
3−content. This expansion can be attributed to the sub-
stitution of relatively small spherical O2−anions ( 140 pm)
with the larger PO4
3−polyanions ( 238 pm), indicating suc-
cessful incorporation into the NCM811 lattice structure [37].
This observation is further corroborated by high-resolution
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Figure
S10), where the (003) lattice spacing increased from 0.481
nm for P0.0-NCM811 to 0.501 nm for P0.3-NCM811, provid-
ing direct evidence of c-axis expansion upon PO4
3−incorpo-
ration. Additionally, the shift of the (003) peak toward lower
angles, consistent with Rietveld results, further supports c-axis
expansion (Figure 2g). Since Li+ primarily diffuse along the
(003) planes within the Li layers, an increased interslab spac-
ing is expected to enhance Li+ mobility, thus, potentially
improving rate capability [38]. The degree of cation mixing,
which can be inferred from the intensity ratio of I(003)/I(104),
also showed a favorable trend [39]. As shown in Table S1, the
I(003)/I(104) ratio increased from 1.80 to 1.92 with higher PO4
3−
content, indicating a decrease in cation mixing. This structural
improvement is expected to contribute to better electrochemi-
cal stability and capacity retention.
To investigate the changes in the surface valence states of
Px-NCM811 cathode materials induced by PO4
3−polyanion
incorporation, XPS analysis was conducted. As shown in
Figure 3a–c, P 2 p spectra of P0.3-NCM811 and P0.5-NCM811
display a prominent peak around 133.4 eV, which is character-
istic of tetrahedral PO4 groups. This observation conﬁrms
the successful incorporation of PO4
3−polyanions into the
NCM811 structure during synthesis. Although the overall
intensity of the P 2 p signal is relatively weak due to the intrin-
sically low phosphorus content, complementary evidence from
XRD peak shifts (Figures 2g) and EDS elemental mapping
(Figures S6–S8) consistently conﬁrms the presence and homo-
geneous distribution of phosphorus. Taken together, these
results substantiate the successful incorporation of PO4
3−poly-
anions into the NCM811 lattice despite the limited signal-to-
noise ratio in the XPS spectra. Figure 3d–f shows the Ni 2p
spectra, where the Ni 2p3/2 and Ni 2p1/2 peaks appear at
approximately 855.1 and 872.5 eV, respectively, along with
their associated satellite peaks. Deconvolution of the Ni 2p3/2
peak reveals contributions from Ni2+ ( 854.6 eV) and Ni3+
(855.6 eV) species. The relative Ni3+ content increases
from 48% (P0.0-NCM811) to 60% (P0.3-NCM811) and 63%
(P0.5-NCM811) with increasing polyanion incorporation, as
summarized in Table S2. This trend suggests that PO4
3−incor-
poration effectively suppresses the surface accumulation of
Ni2+, thereby reducing the degree of cation mixing—consistent
with the ﬁndings from Rietveld reﬁnement analysis. C 1 s spec-
tra for all samples are shown in Figure 3g–i. Peaks observed at
approximately 284.8 and 289.8 eV correspond to hydrocarbon
species and Li2CO3, respectively [40]. The Li2CO3 commonly
originates from residual lithium compounds that react with
atmospheric CO2 and moisture during sample handling, which
is frequently observed in Ni-rich NCM cathodes. Compared to
P0.0-NCM811, both the intensity and relative area of the
Li2CO3 peak decrease signiﬁcantly in the P0.3-NCM811 and
P0.5-NCM811 cathode materials (Table S2), indicating that
PO4
3−incorporation reduces the formation of Li residuals.
This result suggests that during the high-temperature synthesis
process, residual Li on the precursor surface reacts with PO4
3−
species to form a Li3PO4 surface layer. Although the absolute
quantiﬁcation of residual lithium is experimentally challenging
due to its inherently low concentration, the systematic decrease
in the Li2CO3 signal observed in the XPS C 1 s spectra provides
reliable indirect evidence of reduced surface Li. This interpre-
tation is further supported by complementary structural and
compositional analyses, which consistently indicate the stabili-
zation effect of PO4
3−incorporation.
These physicochemical characterizations collectively dem-
onstrate that PO4
3−polyanions are not only incorporated
into the NCM811 lattice, but may also exist as Li3PO4 on the
particle surface. Such dual effects are expected to signiﬁcantly
inﬂuence the electrochemical performance of NCM811 cath-
ode materials, particularly in terms of capacity retention, rate
capability, and long-term cycling stability.

3.2. Electrochemical Performance Evaluation of Px-NCM811
Cathode Materials. The electrochemical performance of
the synthesized Px-NCM811 cathode materials was evaluated
using half-cells assembled with Li metal serving as both
the counter and reference electrodes. Galvanostatic charge–
discharge tests were conducted at 25°C within a potential range
of 3.0–4.5 V (vs. Li/Li+). Figure 4a–d displays the initial
charge–discharge curves measured at a current density of
0.1 C (1.0 C= 180 mA h g−1). Although all Px-NCM811 cath-
ode materials exhibited similar voltage proﬁles, notable differ-
ences in their initial discharge capacities were observed.
Speciﬁcally, the pristine P0.0-NCM811 and P0.5-NCM811 cath-
ode materials delivered an initial discharge capacity of approx-
imately 216 and 215 mA hg−1, respectively, while the P0.3-
NCM811 cathode material, incorporating an optimal amount
of PO4
3−polyanions, showed enhanced capacities of approxi-
mately 227 mA h g−1. Coulombic efﬁciency also improved with
the incorporation of an appropriate amount of polyanions. The
ﬁrst-cycle efﬁciencies of P0.0-NCM811, P0.3-NCM811, and P0.5-
NCM811 were approximately 90.7%, 92.8%, and 90.1%, respec-
tively, indicating that the incorporation of an optimal amount of
PO4
3−positively inﬂuences the electrochemical behavior of the
cathode. Among the cathode materials, P0.3-NCM811 cathode
material exhibited the most favorable combination of initial
capacity and efﬁciency. This enhancement can be correlated
with the XPS results discussed previously. The reduction in
Ni2+ concentration, achieved through PO4
3−incorporation, is
likely to suppress the formation of electrochemically inactive
NiO species on the particle surface, thereby reducing irreversible
capacity loss and improving initial Coulombic efﬁciency.
Figure 4e presents the rate capability of Px-NCM811
cathode materials tested at various current densities (0.2,
0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 C). Compared to the pristine P0.0-
NCM811, both P0.3-NCM811 and P0.5-NCM811 cathode
materials exhibited signiﬁcantly improved rate performance,
with
P0.3-NCM811
demonstrating
the
most
superior
behavior among the series, despite having a lower PO4
3−
content than P0.5-NCM811. Speciﬁcally, the P0.3-NCM811
cathode delivered discharge capacities of approximately
218, 207, 199, 190, and 178 mA h g−1 at current rates of
0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 C, respectively, corresponding to
capacity retentions of approximately 95.8% (0.5 C), 92.6%
(1.0 C), 88.0% (2.0 C), and 82.9% (4.0 C), as shown in
Figure 4f and Figure S11a. Furthermore, as illustrated in
Figure S11b, the P0.3-NCM811 cathode exhibited superior
discharge speciﬁc capacities across all tested C-rates. This
excellent rate performance is attributed to the smaller and
more uniformly distributed primary particles in P0.3-
NCM811, which shorten the Li+ diffusion paths. In addition,
the expanded c-axis resulting from PO4
3−incorporation likely
reduces Li+ diffusion resistance, facilitating more efﬁcient Li+
insertion and extraction during charge–discharge cycles.
Figure 4g illustrates the cycling stability of Px-NCM811
cathodes over 100 cycles at 1.0 C. While all cathode materials
exhibited Coulombic efﬁciencies close to approximately
100%, P0.3-NCM811 and P0.5-NCM811 maintained relatively
higher efﬁciencies throughout the test. Notably, even under a
high cut-off voltage of 4.5V (vs. Li/Li+), P0.3-NCM811 retained
a discharge capacity of approximately 171mAhg−1 after
100 cycles, compared to approximately 146 and 167mAhg−1
for P0.0-NCM811 and P0.5-NCM811, respectively. The corre-
sponding capacity retentions were approximately 74.7%,
86.7%, and 83.2%, further highlighting the superior cycling
stability of P0.3-NCM811. The enhanced long-term stability of
P0.3-NCM811 can be attributed to the reduced cation mixing
and suppression of inactive NiO phase formation, as sup-
ported by both XPS and structural analysis. These synergistic
effects of polyanion incorporation underscore its critical role
in improving both the kinetics and structural stability of Ni-
rich layered cathode materials under high-voltage operation.

3.3. Mechanistic Insights Into the Inﬂuence of PO4
3−
Polyanion Incorporation on the Electrochemical Behavior of
Px-NCM811 Cathode Materials. To elucidate the mechan-
isms underlying the enhanced electrochemical performance
of Px-NCM811 cathode materials upon PO4
3−polyanion
incorporation, a more in-depth comparative analysis was
conducted between P0.0-NCM811 (without PO4
3−) and
P0.3-NCM811, the composition that exhibited the best capac-
ity, rate capability, and cycling stability. Cross-sectional SEM
images of the two cathode materials in the pristine state
(before electrochemical cycling) are shown in Figure 5a,b.
The P0.0-NCM811 cathode material exhibited noticeable
particle fracture and a loosely packed core structure, indicat-
ing lower internal density (Figure 5a). In contrast, the P0.3-
NCM811 cathode material showed no apparent particle
cracking and featured a denser and more compact micro-
structure throughout the particle cross section (Figure 5b).
These observations suggest that PO4
3−incorporation led to a
reduction in the size of primary particles, resulting in a more
densely packed secondary particle architecture. This com-
pact microstructure is expected to suppress particle degrada-
tion and microcrack formation during cycling, contributing
to the improved structural stability observed in P0.3-NCM811.
Furthermore, such dense particle arrangements can reduce
interparticle distances and enhance electronic and ionic con-
ductivity, which likely played a key role in the superior rate
performance exhibited by this composition. Taken together,
these results provide strong evidence that the incorporation
of PO4
3−polyanions not only modiﬁes the bulk lattice
structure and surface chemistry of NCM811, but also signiﬁ-
cantly inﬂuences the internal particle architecture—ultimately
enhancing both the mechanical integrity and electrochemical
performance of the cathode material.
To further elucidate the impact of PO4
3−polyanion
incorporation on the electrochemical behavior of Px-
NCM811 cathode materials, differential capacity (dQ/dV)
analysis
was
performed
for
P0.0-NCM811
and
P0.3-
NCM811 at the 25th, 50th, 75th, and 100th cycles during 1.0
C cycling. As shown in Figure 5c,d, layered NCM cathode
materials undergo sequential phase transitions during Li+
insertion and extraction, following a characteristic sequence
from the original hexagonal phase (H1) to a monoclinic
phase (M), then, to a second hexagonal phase (H2), and
ﬁnally to a third hexagonal phase (H3) (H1 →M →H2 →
H3) [41]. In Ni-rich NCM systems, the H2 →H3 transition
occurring around approximately 4.2 V (vs. Li/Li+) is partic-
ularly critical, often associated with severe structural collapse
and signiﬁcant capacity fading. In the case of P0.0-NCM811,
the anodic peak corresponding to the H2 →H3 transition
progressively shifts to higher potentials with cycling, while
the current intensity notably decreases (Figure 5c). Concur-
rently, the corresponding cathodic peak shifts to lower
potentials, resulting in a voltage gap of approximately 0.27
between the anodic and cathodic peaks by the 100th cycle.
This increasing polarization is indicative of c-axis lattice
shrinkage and elevated internal resistance, likely arising
from particle cracking and interfacial degradation during
repeated cycling. These structural changes may also promote
side reactions between the electrolyte and electrode or the
formation of electrochemically inactive NiO phases. In con-
trast, the P0.3-NCM811 cathode exhibited remarkable stabil-
ity. As shown in Figure 5d, the anodic peak position
remained nearly unchanged even after 100 cycles, and the
associated current decay was minimal. Moreover, the voltage
difference between the anodic and cathodic peaks was sig-
niﬁcantly smaller, around 0.15 V, indicating reduced polari-
zation. These observations strongly suggest that PO4
3−
polyanion incorporation improves the structural reversibility
of the phase transitions, particularly mitigating the detri-
mental effects of the H2 →H3 transition. This enhanced
phase stability is consistent with the improved cycling
performance and reduced impedance observed for P0.3-
NCM811, underscoring the effectiveness of polyanion engi-
neering in stabilizing Ni-rich layered cathode materials.

stability and electrochemical performance of P0.3-NCM811
cathode material, achieved through the appropriate incorpo-
ration of PO4
3−polyanions, arise from their functional effects
within the cathode material system.

A B S T R A C T
This paper focuses on the coupling of the Differential Voltage Analysis (dV/dQ vs Q) and the Distribution of 
Relaxation Times (DRT), whose benefits to electrodes development are illustrated by the tracking of degradation 
effects in the cycling aging of NMC811/Gr coin cells, with and without an NMC electrode coating. The former 
technique allows monitoring of individual electrode loss of active material (LAM), chemistry change and state of 
charge (SOC) alignment of the electrodes, whose variation is related mainly to loss of Li inventory (LLI). DRT is a 
time domain representation of the impedance that offers a high resolution of the resistance processes, com­
plementing the DVA analysis by bringing kinetics degradation of the cells and by identifying in which electrode 
the main resistance processes are originated. The protocol inserting those techniques in an aging regime is a non- 
invasive approach, and it indicated the consumption of Li+ ions as the main reason for capacity decay in both 
cells, with reduced or minimal NMC LAM and chemistry change when coating is applied. From the perspective of 
power decay, the coating decelerated the impedance rise (IR), mostly related to resistance processes in the 
positive electrode.
1. Introduction
The Li-ion batteries (LIBs) have an established competitive position 
in the market of secondary batteries due to its relative high energy 
density and flexible use [1]. However, performance of current 
commercialized chemistries is insufficient for the urgent and growing 
demands of energy storage, notably from the mobility sector [2,3]. 
Electric vehicles are desired to reach longer driving range after complete 
charge, and to be charged faster (<10 min), both attributes that can be 
attained by increasing the energy and power densities of their batteries. 
For this purpose, one of the most applied strategies in the classic LIBs 
that contain liquid organic electrolytes and intercalation electrodes is 
the employ of active materials with superior capacity density and/or 
higher operational voltage [4].
LIBs energy density is mainly limited by the cathodes, primary source 
of Li+ ions, and recent studies have focused on layered transition metal 
oxides known as NMCs (LiNixMnyCo1-x-yO2) for high energy density 
applications [5]. With a potential range compatible to most of the

electrode during operational conditions, which can be attained by using 
electrochemical techniques.
A recurrent tool to evaluate the energy density of a cell is by 
analyzing the area of its V vs Q curve [4,14]. At a fixed temperature and 
quasistatic conditions, the reversible process of lithiation of an inter­
calation material has an open circuit potential that is dependent on the 
difference of chemical potentials of Li in the positive and in the negative 
electrodes. If the counter electrode is an ideal reference electrode, the V 
vs Q curve is then characteristic of the working electrode, and any

The total capacity of each electrode, related to its mass, and their 
effective capacity windows alignment can be tracked in order to follow 
loss of active material (LAM) and loss of Li inventory (LLI) if the full-cell 
curve is successfully reconstructed from its electrodes half-cell curves 
[16–18]. Therefore, the lithiation state of each electrode for a deter­
mined SOC of the full-cell is a parameter to analyze the degradation, and 
the variation of the electrodes lithiation states to the other for a given 
SOC of the cell is referred to as slippage. Inherently, one unique slippage 
is attributed to a cell if two degradation states or states-of-health (SOH) 
are compared, but for practical reasons, it can be monitored at each SOH 
by the non-accessed capacity of one electrode at the end of the full-cell 
discharge (or the offset between the full-cell 0 % SOC and their elec­
trodes 0 % SOC), referred to as the electrode’s slippage [19–21].

DVA captures degradation from close-to-equilibrium conditions, but 
LIBs can be charged or partially charged at relatively high current 
densities, which deviates their V vs Q curves from the OCV curve due to 
ohmic drop and polarization arising from cell impedance [23,24]. These 
kinetic limitations can impose considerable penalty to energy in high 
power application and trigger parasitic reactions while remaining un­
noticed in low current density analysis [23].

Focusing on the electrochemistry analysis of capacity and power 
degradation, the impact of coating a NMC811 (LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2) 
electrode to NMC811/Gr cells capacity retention during cycling is 
studied in terms of thermodynamics and kinetics. For that, DVA and DRT 
are coupled to characterize variations in electrodes V vs Q curves and 
impedance as a function of SOC at different degrees of aging. NMC811/ 
Li, Coated NMC811/Li and Gr/Li have their reversible V vs Q curves and 
DRT peaks as a function of SOC after formation cycles obtained to, 
respectively, reconstruct V vs Q curves and identify electrodes individ­
ual resistance contribution in NMC811/Gr and Coated NMC811/Gr 
cells. LLI could be identified in both cells, with clear differences in 
NMC811 LAM and resistance buildup in absence of the coating, proving 
the approach, still underexploited in battery research [31,32], is very 
useful for monitoring high energy density LIBs cells performance decay.
2. Experimental
2.1. Material description
The uncoated NMC811 electrode was obtained from Umicore, 
Belgium, and it is composed of 95 wt% NMC811 (Umicore), 3 wt% 
carbon black Super C65 (Imerys) and 2 wt% polyvinylidene difluoride 
PVDF (Sigma-Aldrich) in Al foil. It presents 32.6 % of geometric 
porosity, first charge of 1.17 mAh/cm2 (245 mAh/g) and reversible 
discharge of 1.02 mAh/cm2 (213 mAh/g) during formation cycles 
(2.5–4.3V, 25.2 ◦C ± 0.2 ◦C). Coated NMC811 electrode was manufac­
tured in a prototyping scale roll-to-roll coating machine, using 94 wt% 
Coated NMC811 (Umicore), 3 wt% carbon black Super C45 (Imerys) and 
3 wt% polyvinylidene difluoride PVDF (Sigma-Aldrich) in Al foil. It 
presents 37 % of geometric porosity, calculated from the picnometric 
density of the materials, first charge of 1.18 mAh/cm2 (226 mAh/g) and 
reversible discharge of 1.04 mAh/cm2 (199 mAh/g) during formation 
cycles. Gr electrode was obtained from Cidetec, Spain, and it is 
composed of 94 wt% Graphite GHDR 15-4 (Imerys), 2 wt% carbon black 
Super C45 (Imerys), 2 wt% carboxymethylcellulose CMC (Chempoint 
W2000 3 %) and 2 wt% styrene butadiene rubber SBR (Zeon BM451 B 
40 %) in Cu foil. It presented 30 % porosity, first discharge of 1.26 mAh/ 
cm2 (401 mAh/g) and reversible charge of 1.15 mAh/cm2 (366 mAh/g) 
during formation cycles (0.01–1.50V, 25.2 ◦C ± 0.2 ◦C). That way, all 
full-cells had approximately N/P = 1.2 as total capacity ratio between 
negative electrode first reduction and positive electrode first oxidation 
Polypropylene monolayer thin film (Celgard 2500) was employed as 
separator. Electrolyte consisted of 1M LiPF6 in EC:EMC (30 %:70 % wt: 
wt) + 2 wt% VC (E-Lyte, Germany), labeled LP57 + 2 % VC. Li foil 
electrode is 0.38 mm thick (Sigma-Aldrich Chemistry, USA).

2.3. Cells aging protocol
All the cells were aged in a thermal chamber (Binder, Germany) at 
25.2 ◦C ± 0.2 ◦C, and cycled in a VMP2 Potentiostat (BioLogic, France), 
considering 1C = 1 mA/cm2 as current density notation, applied to all 
the cells. Potential windows for cycling were 2.50 V–4.20 V for the full- 
cells, 2.50 V–4.30 V for the positive half-cells, and 1.50 V–0.01 V for the 
negative half-cells. Those cutoff potentials were employed in the for­
mation cycles and in cycling. Since the cells have relatively low loading, 
6h of OCV in the chamber prior to formation cycles were considered 
enough for wettability. Formation cycles consisted of 3 cycles of con­
stant current charge of C/10 and a constant voltage charge with icutoff <
C/50 (CCCV charge), followed by a C/10 discharge, the last discharged 
following a 45 min OCV. In the sequence, full-cells underwent a first 
control step that alternates with 20 aging cycles of CCCV 1C, icutoff < C/ 
20 charge and 1C discharge. The last discharge before each control step 
is at C/10 and followed by 45 min OCV, otherwise charge and discharge 
are separated by 5 min OCV. Control step has an initial C/20 charge, a C/ 
10 discharge +45 min OCV and a PEIS step (200 kHz–10 mHz, ΔV =
7.07 mV rms, 10 points/decade, 3 measures average) that will be 
repeated after each pulse charge of C/20 for 2h until charge cutoff po­
tential is reached. In the end, a 1C discharge enchains the aging cycles. 
Half-cell only passes formation cycles and one control step, with the Gr/ 
Li cells protocol inverting the phase of charge for discharge. Li sym­
metric cell is charged and discharged for the same capacity extent of the 
full-cells (≅1.327 mAh) and using the same current density notation in 
2 cycles of C/10 followed by consecutive pulse C/20 charges for 2 h 
followed by 30 min OCV and PEIS step in the same terms of other cells. 
Each cell was replicated at least twice, being one cell with representative 
SOH evolution at the end of the protocol undergoing further DVA and 
DRT analysis.
2.4. DVA fitting
The OCV V vs Q and dV/dQ vs Q curves of NMC811/Li charge and 
Gr/Li discharge at C/20 are obtained during the initial half of the half- 
cells control step. Using the Electrochemistry Visualization Application 
free software (EVA) [33], those curves are used to fit the experimental 
full-cell data and evaluate its electrodes masses and alignment (slippage) 
for each control step. As this fitting model is based on capturing the Li 
(de)insertion curves of the materials, parasitic reactions should be 
minimized, turning at first contradictory the choice of the charge 
(discharge for Gr/Li) over the discharge profile, as it is in the former that 
resides most of the reported degradation mechanisms [34]. However, it 
was taken to avoid the variation of Gr/Li delithiation V vs Q curve to the 
decreasing initial lithiation state, expected to occur in case of Li+ ions 
consumption in the course of cycling [35]. To avoid residual kinetic 
interferences from the cycles with higher currents, a C/10 discharge was 
applied before all the control steps. As the model also considers elec­
trodes in series, they presented the same diameter to prevent inhomo­
geneous reaction in the overhang [36,37].
2.5. Distribution of relaxation times
DRT is converted from the SOC dependent PEIS of all cells using the 
RelaxIS3 Impedance Spectrum Analysis software (RHD Instruments, 
Germany). Low frequency mathematical fitting and subtraction are 
applied to PEIS at 0 % SOC, meanwhile in other SOCs such procedures 
were not applied, being the DRT in the frequency of interest restricted to 
the range 200 kHz–0.2 Hz. Regularization parameter λ [38] was adopted 
as 10−3 for all cells and 10−2 for the Li symmetric cell.

3.2. Changes in the OCV V vs Q curves using differential voltage analysis
In Fig. 2, the V vs Q and its dV/dQ vs Q fittings are presented, with 
the vertical line in Q = 0 marking the beginning of the full-cell charge, 
reference for the electrodes relative capacities. One electrode’s slippage 
is then the amount of negative capacity relative to that mark. Comparing 
the OCV curves of NMC811 and Gr in the beginning and in the end of the 
aging, from the fittings in Fig. 2a to Fig. 2b, a clear increase of the 
positive electrode slippage exposes that the mechanisms of capacity fade 
result in LLI. For later states of aging, an OCV curve of the NMC811 
electrode does not maintain its shape, specifically at potentials above 
4.2 V, hindering complete fitting at high SOCs. Those changes reflect in 
partial suppression of capacity exchange, being referred to as NMC811 
undercharge. For the Coated NMC811/Gr cell, from Fig. 2c to Fig. 2d, 
the LLI is equally spotted by the increase in the positive slippage and by 
the decrease of the initial negative slippage. The latter is present if the 
negative electrode is incompletely delithiated when the cell is dis­
charged. Li+ ions reduction in the interface electrode/electrolyte of the 
Gr electrode, forming a passivation layer is the major reported cause for 
LLI in systems containing that active material. An initially large LLI is 
expected for the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation during the 
first cycles, followed by its increase upon aging [39,40]. Concerning the 
undercharge effect, differently from the NMC811/Gr, no undercharge 
was observed for this cell, which indicates that the coating prevents or 
minimizes mechanisms related to it.
DVA fitting qualitative measurements allow tracking not only of 
electrode’s slippage but also of its mass variation during all the moni­
tored control steps. Between the first and the last control steps of the 
NMC811/Gr and the Coated NMC811/Gr cells, the loss of capacity was 
0.192 mAh out of 1.420 mAh and 0.126 mAh out of 1.277 mAh, 
respectively. In Fig. 3a, 3,8 % of NMC811 LAM was observed around the 
cycle 108, meanwhile a minimal 0,3 % LAM of Gr was detected in the 
last control step. Fig. 3b indicated that the magnitude of the under­
charge is secondary to the LLI and it appears after NMC811 LAM is 
detected. It is important to add that the positive slippage is reduced in 
the third control step, when LAM takes place, which is an indicative that 
the lost NMC811 fraction was predominantly poorly lithiated, when the 
cell is at high SOC. LAM of Coated NMC811 in Fig. 3c is negligible, 
meaning that the coating impacted also the degradation mechanisms 
linked to that effect. The initial increase of active Gr mass was spotted in 
some similar cells and is hypothesized to come from incomplete elec­
trode wetting during initial cycles, possibly at the source of their noisy 
charge/discharge capacity values. In Fig. 3d a smoother evolution of the 
slippages is related to the absence of LAM. Full access of fitting images of 
all control steps is available in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2 of the Supporting 
Information for the NMC811/Gr and Coated NMC811/Gr cells 
respectively.

3.4. Prospective effects of coating in degradation mechanisms
NMC811 LAM was minimized after the coating and, based on the 
results of DVA and DRT, it is suggested for the analyzed NMC811/Gr 
cells a synergy of the most reported mechanisms leading to this effect: 
particle cracking and inactive species formation on the electrode/elec­
trolyte interface. Ni-rich NMCs during the delithiation experience 
anisotropic lattice expansion followed by a contraction around 4.2 V 
that eventually lead to strain in the boundaries of primary particles, and 
thus cracks in the secondary particles [10,49]. Cracks themselves do not 
cause LAM if not followed by electrical disconnection of the particles 
from the current collector, and their formation can reverse during the 
process of lithiation in the initial cycles. However, they promote elec­
trolyte permeation in the electrode, and the reactivity in this interface. 
Conversion of NMC into spinel and rock-salt insulant structures results 
in direct LAM and in the observed charge transfer increase [49,50]. 
Observed undercharge seen at high SOCs is related to interfacial strain of 
thick rock-salt layers that hinder NMC from contraction at the end of the 
charge, finally changing its OCV curve, even at low current densities 
[51]. Addition of coating is proposed to act by turning the electrode 
surface less reactive to electrolyte and by hindering the capacity ex­
change when the positive electrode is at potentials above 4.2V, which 
turns the structure collapse milder and reduces electrolyte permeation. 
The capacity penalty of the coating is compensated by the deaccelera­
tion of electrode inactivation and impedance rise, quantified in (Fig. 8). 
Resistance processes of both cells in early, moderate and late aging states 
of the cycling were obtained by the areas of each DRT peak between two 
local minima.

4. Conclusion
The impact of coating the positive electrode was analyzed by 
comparing a NMC811/Gr and a coated NMC811/Gr cell, using electro­
chemical techniques. The application of an aging protocol coupling DVA 
fitting and DRT techniques was shown to be a non-invasive method to 
track degradation effects during cell operation, in terms of the ther­
modynamics and the kinetics from each electrode. The coating limited 
the NMC811 electrode delithiation to potentials above 4.2V, but this 
reduced capacity, as proposed in this work, smoothed volume variation 
and thus particle cracking and electrolyte permeation. Therefore, it 
minimized active material conversion to spinel and rock-salt formation, 
sources of LAM, undercharge at high SOC and positive electrode charge 
transfer resistance increase, which increased the capacity and power 
retention of the cell during cycling. The suggested processes were based 
on literature-reported degradation mechanisms for the studied mate­
rials, whose effects were closely aligned with the observed NMC811 loss 
of mass and charge transfer increase, more pronounced at high SOC. 
Although only other techniques such as XRD, XPS, SEM and others can 
confirm, DVA coupled with DRT succeeds in providing robust insights 
into LLI, LAM and impedance rise in each electrode. One of the detailed 
pieces of information obtained from the used approach was relating the 
decrease in the resistance of the coated NMC811/Gr cell in low SOCs at 
aging advances to the positive electrode slippage. This virtual power 
capability improvement in the discharged cell came from the growing 
absence of the NMC811 with high levels of lithiation, highly resistive, 
from the effective capacity window of the cell as LLI increases, high­

electrodes slippages. The presented electrochemistry methodology 
found useful application in evaluating the impact of a NMC811 coating 
on the capacity and resistance of a cell during different stages of its life 
cycle, non-invasively and in operando conditions. All those character­

process of optimizing electrodes coatings and can be equally employed 
to compare cell general degradation under different parameters such as

Abstract
Ni-rich layered oxide cathode materials are promising candidates for high-specific-energy battery systems owing 
to their high reversible capacity. However, their widespread application is still severely impeded by severe capacity 
loss upon long-term cycling. It has been proven that the cyclic stability of Ni-rich cathode materials is closely 
related to their microstructure and morphology. Despite this, the influence of the microstructure of primary 
particles on the fatigue mechanism of Ni-rich cathode materials during prolonged cycling has not been fully 
understood. Here, two Ni-rich layered spherical agglomerate oxides consisting of the primary particle with different 
length/width ratios are successfully synthesized. It is found that the long-term structural stability of both materials 
strongly depends on the microstructure of primary crystallites, although there is no significant difference between 
the electrochemical and crystalline characteristics during the initial cycle. A higher primary particle length/width 
ratio could effectively inhibit the accumulation of microcracks and chemical degradation during long-term cycling, 
thereby promoting the electrochemical performance of the cathode materials (80% capacity retention after

200 cycles at 1 C compared to the 55% of the counterpart with a lower primary particle length/width ratio). This 
study highlights the structure-activity relationship between the primary particle microstructure and fatigue 
mechanisms during long-term cycling, thereby advancing the development of Ni-rich cathode materials.

INTRODUCTION
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) have gained wide popularity in renewable energy-related fields owing to their 
numerous advantages, including high energy density, cost-effectiveness, and safety[1]. Nevertheless, the 
immense demand for high-specific-energy LIBs necessitates further developments, especially in the areas of 
electric vehicles (EVs) and energy storage systems. The urgent need to explore high-capacity cathode 
materials has arisen since the energy density and cost of LIBs are chiefly limited by cathode materials[2]. Ni-
rich layered oxides, such as LiNixCoyMnzO2 (NCM, where x + y + z = 1 and x ≥ 0.8) and LiNixCoyAlzO2 
(NCA), are viewed as potential cathode materials for the next-generation LIBs owing to their high discharge 
capacity (> 200 mAh g-1), high working potential, and low manufacture cost[3,4]. However, the increased 
nickel content in NCM or NCA also poses severe chemical and structural instability issues upon long-term 
cycling, mainly originating from accumulations of the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI), electrolyte 
decomposition, formation of inactive rock-salt phase on the particle surface, and propagation of 
microcracks induced by the anisotropic unit cell volume expansion/contraction[5,6].
Tremendous endeavor has been devoted to enhancing the cycling stability of Ni-rich cathode materials, e.g., 
doping[7,8], surface coating[9,10], concentration gradients, and single-crystalline morphology[11]. Furthermore, 
considerable attention has been given to the physical/chemical features of primary particles (e.g., size, 
morphology, arrangement, etc.), as they significantly influence the electrochemical performance of cathode 
materials[12]. Consequently, numerous reports have emerged concerning microstructure texture, with the 
objective of mitigating structural degradation and enhancing the cycling stability of Ni-rich layered cathode 
materials. Introducing low-solubility dopants to refine primary particle size serves to dissipate the local 
internal strain caused by abrupt lattice collapse, thereby suppressing the propagation of microcracks and 
strengthening the mechanical integrity of cathode materials[13]. Moreover, the manipulation of target lattice 
facet orientation in primary particles, aiming to elevate the oxygen stability or improve ion transfer kinetics, 
represents an effective strategy in the engineering of Ni-rich cathodes[14]. While numerous reports have 
focused on element doping with surface energy modification (such as B, Mo, Nb, etc.) to tailor the primary 
particle, this approach often results in sacrificed available capacity and increased process cost[15-17]. In 
contrast, there has been limited research on regulating microstructure through adjusting processing 
conditions, which offers the unique advantage of inheriting the particle structure characteristics from the 
precursor to the final cathode materials. Furthermore, the interrelated mechanism between the cathode 
material primary particle length/width ratio and the capacity decay upon prolonged cycling remains 
unclear.
In this work, [Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1](OH)2 precursors and corresponding Li[Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1]O2 (NCM811) cathode 
materials with two distinct primary particle length/width ratios were synthesized by manipulating the co-
precipitation synthetic conditions. The electrochemical performance properties, structural evolution, and 
fatigue mechanism of NCM811 were thoroughly investigated via in situ X-ray radiation diffraction (XRD), 
electrochemical tests, and post-mortem analysis. During the initial lithiation/delithiation process, both 
cathode materials exhibited similar discharge specific capacity and lattice parameter evolution. However, a

higher primary particle length/width ratio demonstrated an effective alleviation of the propagation of 
microcracks, thus mitigating accumulated surface chemical degradation and improving phase transition 
reversibility. This led to superior cycling stability and structural reversibility for the Ni-rich cathode with a 
higher primary particle length/width ratio (namely, HR-NCM), as evidenced by an impressive capacity 
retention of 80% after 200 cycles at 1 C. In contrast, the oxide with the lower length/width ratio (LR-NCM) 
exhibited a capacity retention of only 55%. This work sheds light on the relationship between primary 
particle length/width ratios and long-term cycling stability of Ni-rich cathode materials. These findings are 
significant in guiding the design of desired high-specific-energy LIBs.
EXPERIMENTAL
Synthesis of materials
The hydroxide precursors [Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1](OH)2 were synthesized via a co-precipitation method. Transition 
metal sulfates, including NiSO4·6H2O, CoSO4·7H2O, and MnSO4·H2O, in molar ratios of Ni/Co/Mn = 8:1:1 
were slowly added into a continuously stirred tank reactor with NH3·H2O as a chelating agent. To maintain 
the pH level, a 4 M NaOH (aq.) solution was simultaneously pumped into the reactor. The pH was 
controlled within the range of 10.95-11.05 for HR-precursor synthesis and 11.40-11.50 for LR-precursor 
synthesis. N2 gas was continuously fed into the reactor to maintain an inert atmosphere during synthesis. 
The synthesized precursors were collected via vacuum filtration, washed with deionized water, and dried in 
an oven at 110 °C for 24 h. The as-synthesized precursors were then mixed thoroughly with a specific 
amount of LiOH·H2O with a molar ratio of 1:1.05 (the excess lithium was used to compensate for lithium 
loss during the calcination process), preheated at 500 °C for 6 h, and calcined at 780 °C for 12 h in a pure O2 
atmosphere to produce the LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 cathode materials.
Characterization
The chemical composition of synthesized oxide materials was analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma 
atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES, Agilent 7800). The crystal structure of the cathode powder 
samples was determined using ex situ high-resolution synchrotron-based XRD (sXRD) at beamline P02.1 
with synchrotron radiation energy of 60 keV (λ = 0.2072 Å) at the DESY (Deutsches Elektronen 
synchrotron) in Hamburg, Germany. The crystal structure evolution of the cathodes during the initial 
charge-discharge process was analyzed using in situ XRD with Ag Kα radiation (Bruker D8 ADVANCE, 
λ = 0.5594 Å), and the crystallographic parameters were obtained using the Rietveld method and FullProf 
program. XRD with Cu Kα radiation (Bruker D8 ADVANCE, λ = 1.5406 Å) was used to characterize the 
crystal structures of precursors and cathode electrodes before and after cycling. The morphologies and 
microstructures of the precursors and cathode materials were observed using scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM, Zeiss Merlin), and cross-section SEM samples were prepared with a cross-section polisher (CP, IB-
19510 JEOL). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) 
mapping images were captured using JEOL JEM-2100 equipment operating at 200 keV. The surface 
chemistry of the electrodes was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K
α spectrometer). Finally, all cycled electrode samples were rinsed with diethyl carbonate (DEC) solvents to 
remove residual electrolyte salts and by-products before the relevant characteristics were analyzed.
Electrochemical tests
To fabricate the cathode electrodes, a mixture of cathode material powders, super P, and poly(vinylidene 
fluoride) in a weight ratio of 80:10:10 was fully blended in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone. The resulting slurry was 
applied onto aluminum foil and dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h. Lithium metal cells were constructed 
using CR-2032 coin cells in an Ar-filled glovebox with the cathode and lithium metal foils serving as the 
counter and reference electrode, respectively. The electrolyte used was 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC/DMC 
(1:1:1 Vol%) with 5 wt% FEC as an additive. The electrochemical cycling performance of the cells was tested

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The hydroxide precursors with different primary particle sizes were obtained using a typical co-
precipitation synthesis method, and details of the preparation process are in the Supporting Information. 
Supplementary Figure 1 illustrates that the XRD patterns of the obtained precursors were similar to the 
standard spectra of β-Ni(OH)2 (PDF#14-0117). All diffraction reflections could be indexed to a hexagonal 
structure with a space group of P-3m1[18]. In addition to this, SEM analysis characterized the morphology of 
the synthesized precursors. The spherical-like morphology of the secondary particles in both precursors, 
composed of nanosheet primary particles, was evident in Supplementary Figure 2, Figure 1A and B. While 
the width of primary particles in the LR-precursor was noticeably larger than that in the HR-precursor, this 
difference can be attributed to the competition between nucleation and complexation under different co-
precipitation pH conditions[13]. The growth process of hydroxide precursors can be divided into three main 
stages, including nucleation and growth of single crystalline grains, agglomeration to form primary 
particles, and the subsequent growth of secondary particles. The higher pH condition accelerates the 
nucleation process and promotes the formation of hydroxide crystal nuclei. These nuclei then agglomerate 
on the surface of as-formed primary particles, which finally construct thicker primary particles within the 
LR-precursor.
The precursors were further mixed with lithium sources to synthesize the Ni-rich layered oxides via high-
temperature lithiation reactions. The composition of HR-NCM and LR-NCM was verified using ICP-AES, 
revealing that the Ni, Co, and Mn atom ratios aligned with the target compositions 
[Supplementary Table 1]. Figure 1C and D shows the XRD patterns of HR-NCM and LR-NCM cathodes 
and corresponding Rietveld refinement plots. All the diffraction peaks can be well indexed as hexagonal α-
NaFeO2 layered structures belonging to the R-3m space group without any extra reflections denoting an 
impurity phase[19]. Notably, the XRD patterns showed a clear splitting between 006/102 and 108/110 
reflections, implying a well-ordered layered structure for both samples [Supplementary Figure 3]. Moreover, 
the integrated intensity ratio of I003/I104 in the two oxides was greater than 1.2 [Supplementary Figure 4], 
indicating negligible cation mixing[20]. These results align with the degree of Li+/Ni2+ mixing in the Li layer, 
as determined from the refinement results (i.e., 1.31% for HR-NCM and 1.34% for LR-NCM, 
Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, the elemental mappings depicted in 
Supplementary Figures 5 and 6 demonstrate the homogeneous distribution of Ni, Co, Mn, and O in both 
cathodes at the micrometer scale. Notably, the materials exhibit the formation of micro-sized secondary 
particles with tightly-packed nanosized primary particles, as illustrated by the inserted SEM images in 
Figure 1C and D. Furthermore, the size distribution of primary particles in Ni-rich layered oxides was 
obtained by measuring the length and width of single primary particles one by one in ImageJ software. The 
statistical analysis in Figure 1E and F reveals a higher average length/width ratio distribution of primary 
particles for HR-NCM (2.2) than for LR-NCM (1.7), which is highly related to the primary particle size 
characteristics in their precursor. As depicted in Figure 1G, a high-resolution TEM image of HR-NCM 
displays an average interplanar distance of approximately 0.47 nm, corresponding to the (003) crystal plane 
of the layered structure (R-3m)[21].

specific capacity at 0.1 C, which is approximately 200 mAh g-1 (204.6 mAh g-1 for HR-NCM and 
203.7 mAh g-1 for LR-NCM). The corresponding dQ dV-1 curves were obtained by differentiating the initial 
charge and discharge profiles [Supplementary Figure 7]. As the cathodes underwent lithiation/delithiation, 
they experienced a series of phase transitions associated with four distinct redox peaks (hexagonal (H1) → 
monoclinic (M) → hexagonal (H2) → hexagonal (H3) phase)[22]. CV tests were also conducted to examine 
the redox reactions and phase transitions that occur during the charge/discharge processes. 
Supplementary Figure 8 illustrates that both cathodes exhibit comparable redox behaviors consistent with 
the dQ dV-1 curves. The dissimilarity in anodic peaks during the initial cycle contributed to the formation of 
the CEI[23]. The electrochemical polarization and irreversible reaction correspond to the potential intervals 
(ΔV) between the H1↔M reduction/oxidation peaks. In the HR-NCM cathode, the ΔV is 0.09 V, which is 
smaller than that of the LR-NCM (0.16 V), indicating smaller polarization during the initial scanning for the 
HR-NCM.

The evolution of the structure and lattice parameters of NCM cathodes is not only dependent on the crystal 
structure properties of materials but also regulated by the microstructure texture of material particles. Thus, 
in situ XRD analysis was further performed on Li/HR-NCM and Li/LR-NCM cells during the initial cycle to 
conduct an accurate study on the structural evolution of the cathode materials with distinct primary particle 
length/width ratios during the electrochemical process. The in situ XRD patterns of both cathodes exhibited 
a similar evolution of reflection shift during cycling [Supplementary Figures 13 and 14]. Charge-discharge 
curves and contour maps of 003 and 110 reflection (representing the c and α lattice parameter, respectively) 
were presented in Figure 3A and B. For both cathodes, the 110 reflection gradually shifts towards higher 
angles during the charging process, indicating the monotonous decrease in the α lattice parameter due to 
the oxidation of transition metals and decreased ionic radius in the transition metal layers[14,28]. At a low 
delithiation state, the 003 reflection slightly shifts to a lower angle, indicating the expansion of the c lattice 
parameter owing to the intensified electrostatic repulsive force between neighboring oxygen slabs when the 
Li ions deintercalated from the Li layers[29]. Upon further charging, the 003 reflection markedly shifts to a 
higher angle owing to eliminated pillar effects along with the reduced electrostatic repulsion triggered by the 
increase of Ni4+-O2- covalent bonding strength[30], indicating the contraction of the unit cell along the c-axis. 
Furthermore, Figure 3C-E plots the change in α, c, and unit cell volume of the cathode materials as a 
function of the SOC during the charging process. The α lattice parameter varied by approximately 1.9% for 
both cathodes in the initial charge process. There is no discernible difference in the maximum c lattice 
parameter contraction between both cathodes (2.2% for HR-NCM and 2.1% for LR-NCM). These results 
suggest that the length/width ratio of the primary particles has no significant effect on the initial crystal 
structural evolution of the synthesized cathode materials. It is important to note that the evolution of lattice 
parameters during the charge/discharge process was highly anisotropic, which could potentially result in the 
generation and propagation of microcracks after prolonged cycling[31,32]. Therefore, more research is needed 
to understand the impact of primary particle length/width ratios on the structural stability of cathodes.

from 14.1930 Å to 14.2025 Å) was smaller than that of LR-NCM (0.31°, corresponding to the increased c 
from 14.2017 Å to 14.3132 Å). Moreover, the splitting of 006/102 reflections for HR-NCM was well retained

electrodes [Figure 4D], while only a few microcracks were present in the cycled HR-NCM cathode 
[Figure 4C]. The presence of microcracks propagated to the outside surface of particles provides numerous 
channels for electrolyte penetration to the inner particles. This allows the electrolyte to react with the newly 
exposed active cathode substances and aggravates more parasitic reactions[34]. Such reactions generally 
induce extensive generation of an inactivated NiO-like rock-salt phase and a CEI layer with transition metal 
dissolution, resulting in capacity fading and structural collapse upon cycling[35]. Besides, the difference in the 
primary particle length/width ratios also reflects the relative exposure of crystal planes with varying 
chemical stability to resist the oxygen vacancy formation and side reactions with electrolytes, which 
influences the stability of the cathode materials[36].

structural evolution during the initial charge/discharge process of cathodes with various primary particle 
length/width ratios have no significant difference. However, the regulated microstructure could effectively 
alleviate the accumulation of microcracks and consequent electrolyte penetration during the prolonged 
cycling, which results in the surface chemical damage and bulk crystal structure degradation. As a result, the 
cathode with a higher length/width ratio presented superior capacity retention during long-term cycling.
CONCLUSIONS
In summary, the Ni-rich layered cathode materials with different primary particle length/width ratios were 
successfully synthesized by adjusting the preparation conditions. The electrochemical capacity and lattice 
parameter evolution during the initial charge/discharge process were not significantly affected by the 
Page 11 of 
Han et al. Energy Mater 2024;4:400001
https://dx.doi.org/10.20517/energymater.2023.59
13
difference of primary particle microstructures in cathode materials. However, the cathode materials with a 
higher primary particle length/width ratio exhibited superior capacity retention upon long-term cycling. In 
detail, the regulated primary particle microstructure effectively inhibited the generation and propagation of 
microcracks in fatigued material particles. Thus, the strengthened mechanical integrity of cathode materials 
significantly reduced the accumulation of surface chemistry degradation and bulk crystal structural damage 
during repeat cycling. As a result, the NCM811 material with a higher primary particle length/width ratio 
maintained considerable Li+ insertion/extraction reversibility and ion diffusion/transfer kinetics, 
contributing to excellent reversible discharge specific capacity retention (160 mAh g-1 after 200 cycles at 1 C 
rate). This work highlights the importance of microstructure modulation on the cycling stability promotion 
of Ni-rich layered oxides to design state-of-the-art high-specific-energy LIB systems.
